OpenAI says it has disproved a conjecture that underpins modern complexity theory

Following last month's run of machine generated proofs, OpenAI has added a claimed disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a generalisation of a 1991 local smoothing conjecture to every dimension, and partial progress toward an existence proof for four dimensional quantum field theories including pure Yang Mills, which is a Millennium Prize problem. If the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false, some cryptographic schemes may have shortcuts faster than brute force. Anthropic researcher Levent Alpoge says his lab reached the same conclusion independently about two weeks earlier, and Greg Brockman cautioned that P still probably does not equal NP. Ryan Williams is organising an open examination of the refutation with no non disclosure agreements, and OpenAI says it is seeking no academic credit.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 27 October 2026, What Anthropic's IPO Filing Reveals

The problem of proofs no human can read did not start with AI

Kevin Hartnett told Machine Learning Street Talk that mathematics hit the limits of human refereeing long before language models arrived. Thomas Hales finished his proof of the Kepler conjecture in 1998 leaning so heavily on code that the Annals of Mathematics could not state whether it was true, and only the later Flyspeck project produced a machine checked version. Hartnett traces the path from there through Lean, Kevin Buzzard's worry that his own field was becoming a house of cards, and Peter Scholze's Liquid Tensor Experiment. The idea he calls the formalisation gap, the distance between the mathematics a human means and the statement a checker actually certifies, is the one worth carrying into this week's OpenAI claims.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 26 October 2026, Mathematics Just Changed Forever, Kevin Hartnett

Steve Eisman is not buying the Anthropic IPO

The investor known from The Big Short gave Ed Elson his read of the S-1 and said plainly that he is not buying. His objections run to corporate governance and to which numbers in the filing investors should actually be watching, rather than to the revenue line itself. The governance question is live because Anthropic's stated corporate purpose in the prospectus is the responsible development of advanced AI for the long term benefit of humanity, which sits awkwardly beside a fiduciary duty to public shareholders. Elson closed the episode arguing the opposite case, that the IPO is misunderstood.

Anthropic's founders keep control of the company after it lists

The filing creates a new founders class of stock, modelled on the structure Mark Zuckerberg used, giving the co-founders 50.1 percent voting control over key decisions including board selection. The financing behind it is similarly layered: 6.2 billion dollars of Amazon convertible notes, up to 77 billion dollars of promissory notes from Amazon, Broadcom and Google, a 15 billion dollar Morgan Stanley revolver, and Broadcom tied vehicles of 35 billion and now 63 billion dollars structured with Apollo and Blackstone. Anthropic gave no clear total addressable market figure, instead valuing global knowledge work at 40 trillion dollars. It also pledged to keep Claude free of advertising, saying it has chosen not to introduce that pressure into the product.

Wall Street is pricing Anthropic's growth and skipping its own risk language

Decoded by POLITICO took the one part of the prospectus nobody else is pricing: Dario Amodei has told investors his lab's models could threaten humanity, and that language is now in a filing for what may be the largest listing ever. The episode's argument is that public markets are far less troubled by existential risk than by risks with a clear line to the income statement, so the disclosure reads as boilerplate rather than as a warning. Nathan Calvin of Encode AI joined to discuss his recent exchange with Amodei on X and his reaction to the filings. It is the sharpest framing of the IPO story this week.

OpenAI has stood up an election operations centre for the midterms

OpenAI told Decoded by POLITICO that it is running an Election Operations Center from this week until every midterm vote is counted, staffed by hundreds of people across multiple teams and led by Josh Lawson, its head of civic and strategic risk. The team watches for misinformation and election disruption in real time, particularly abuse of OpenAI's own tools, and monitors the company's May integrations with Democracy Works for registration and polling data and the Associated Press for live results. One concrete mechanism is what the company calls cartoonification: ask for a fake photograph of a long queue at a polling place and the model returns a cartoon instead. A Brennan Center report last week found ChatGPT was the only one of six popular AI tools to have made significant progress against election disinformation since May.

Kimi K3.1 Pro puts the Chinese frontier about five weeks behind

Moonshot's first update to the K3 family ranks fourth on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index with a score of 55, sitting just behind Opus 5.5 on GDPVal-AA and Terminal Bench 4.0 and level with Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol. Moonshot stripped cyber data from K3.1 Pro's training and Artificial Analysis still scores it at 64 percent on its cyber index, the highest of any publicly available model and five points behind GPT-6 Sol in Daybreak Blue. OpenAI argues computer use is about to pass expert humans on speed

Ari Weinstein and James Sun of OpenAI told the a16z podcast that computer use is entering its scaling moment, driven by better models, faster execution and stronger safety controls, and that agents are approaching the point of operating a computer faster than an expert can. The consequence they draw is that the graphical interface becomes a universal integration surface, removing the need for APIs or bespoke connectors, which both extends the life of legacy software and speeds up new applications. They cite agents already working inside legacy ERP systems, reconciling financial data and handling procurement, and argue the harness around a model now matters as much as the model's raw intelligence. The episode is paired with a16z research promoting the same thesis and the firm invests in agent infrastructure, so the framing serves both the guest's roadmap and the host's book.

Source: the a16z Podcast, 26 October 2026, OpenAI: When AI Agents Use Computers Faster Than You

Supabase is rebuilding the database around the assumption that an agent is driving

Paul Copplestone and Ant Wilson told Latent Space that Supabase Compute is in private alpha, letting teams run work such as document chunking and embedding generation next to the database, with logs, checks and connection context exposed so an agent can understand what it is changing. A Docker free local runtime is also in alpha, and declarative schemas put the desired database state into Git where an agent can read, edit and fork it, which swyx compares to a Terraform moment for the database. Multigres, a highly available Postgres, is in private alpha and the company has acquired Turso. Supabase says it now launches about five million databases a month and is planning for what happens if agents push that toward billions of applications.

Source: Latent Space, 28 October 2026, Why Agents Love Supabase
