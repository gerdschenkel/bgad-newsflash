OpenAI buys Orchestra Bio and folds it into its Rosalind life sciences suite

The first substantive public conversation with the people running OpenAI's life sciences push came days after the company acquired Orchestra Bio, whose founder Ashoka Rajendra is now at OpenAI and on the show alongside Joy Jiao, who leads its life sciences model work. Orchestra's agentic drug program layer is being folded into Rosalind, OpenAI's life sciences product, extending it from research support into the operational side of running a drug program. The acquisition has been widely reported since, so the episode is useful mainly for the strategy behind it: train the best model, build a product layer for scientists, then prove the capability through outside collaborations. Both hosts are a16z Bio and Health partners and the firm invests across this field, so treat the framing as a thesis rather than a neutral read.

Source: The a16z Show, 23 November 2026, The One-Person Biotech: OpenAI Life Sciences

Two thousand agents screened two thousand gene edits across eight million cells

Jiao's central claim is that biology's bottleneck is no longer data but human attention, and that agents remove the ceiling: for the first time, she says, researchers are not limited by how much they can personally read. The proof point offered is a collaboration with Perturb AI in which roughly 2,000 agents analysed about 2,000 genetic perturbations across eight million cells in a mouse brain screen, surfacing patterns human researchers had missed over months. She also predicts AI molecular design changes substantially within three to six months, with foundational breakthroughs in structural biology plausible inside about two years. The specific figures come from reporting on the episode rather than from the published show notes, so treat the numbers as indicative.

Source: The a16z Show, 23 November 2026, The One-Person Biotech: OpenAI Life Sciences

OpenAI's biosecurity answer is to check who you work for, not what you ask

Rather than trying to infer intent from prompts, OpenAI verifies a user's institutional affiliation and then leans partly on that employer's own lab oversight, a posture Rajendra described as trusted access. He put the demand case at 330 million of ChatGPT's 1.2 billion users asking health questions, and cited a Boston Children's Hospital collaboration that identified the cause of a rare disease for a patient who had waited twenty years for a diagnosis. The episode's own caveat is worth keeping: the strongest evidence for agent scale discovery still comes from curated collaborations, not from anything a customer can buy today.

Source: The a16z Show, 23 November 2026, The One-Person Biotech: OpenAI Life Sciences

Biden's AI advisors put the allied compute lead over China at about 100 to 1

Ben Buchanan, the first White House Special Advisor for AI, gave the clearest public numbers yet on the thinking behind US export controls: the allied bloc produces compute at roughly a hundred to one advantage over China, but China's effective access gap is only about fifteen to one. Closing remote access and smuggling routes, he argued, is what would turn the production advantage into a real access advantage. The explicit theory of the case was to build a lead through the compute chokehold and then, in his phrase, spend that lead on safety rather than on pure acceleration. He and co-author Tantum Collins have just published a book on the same argument.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 21 November 2026, Spend the Lead on Safety: Ben Buchanan and Tantum Collins on the Fate of the World

The Lima summit produced a US and China agreement that humans, not AI, control nuclear weapons

Buchanan and Collins gave the first detailed account of the US and China AI diplomacy record they ran. Woodside in November 2023 produced agreement to set up an AI expert group; Geneva in May 2024 went badly, with the Chinese side opening by asking the United States to sell it chips and treating risk discussion as a plot of some kind; Lima in November 2024 produced the agreement that humans rather than AI should control nuclear weapons. Collins noted the wording covers only nuclear employment decisions and took months to narrow to that. Buchanan cited the INF Treaty as the model: negotiate from strength, not from parity.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 21 November 2026, Spend the Lead on Safety: Ben Buchanan and Tantum Collins on the Fate of the World

A former White House AI advisor says he would ban closed loop recursive self improvement outright

The regulatory package Buchanan and Collins proposed is more specific than most: mandatory pre-deployment testing, government evaluators embedded inside the labs, a government mechanism to pace the frontier, and state help securing labs against theft. Buchanan said he would ban closed loop recursive self improvement, and argued that date based pauses are a mistake because they build a compute overhang, preferring thresholds instead. Host Nathan Labenz pushed back that Anthropic's own Responsible Scaling Policy has been moving away from threshold commitments. The timing is notable: OpenAI's chief scientist published an essay over the weekend calling for industry wide limits on model capability.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 21 November 2026, Spend the Lead on Safety: Ben Buchanan and Tantum Collins on the Fate of the World

The first real AI emergency will be cyber or bio, and labs have already armed the defenders

Asked where the first genuine emergency comes from, Buchanan named cyber or bio misuse rather than a general capability jump, pointing to a North Korean bank attack and to the risk that a model breakout becomes an enduring swarm of agents rather than a single incident. He credited the frontier labs, Anthropic among them, with giving defenders access to the same class of cyber capability, and said he does not believe China would have done the same. On open weights he defended the decision not to restrict releases, and said he expects the riskiest open weight model to come out of China.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 21 November 2026, Spend the Lead on Safety: Ben Buchanan and Tantum Collins on the Fate of the World

Governments are now announcing about fifty new AI agent deployments a week

Luukas Ilves, co-founder of The Agentic State and formerly Estonia's government CIO, said the initiative's observatory is logging roughly fifty public announcements of government agent deployments every week. The worked example was New York City's sidewalk cafe permits, where the city Comptroller found more than half of restaurants in the outdoor dining program waited over nineteen months for approval. Eight of the nine steps are form filling and error correction, including a requirement for 3D renderings, so Ilves argued agent assisted checks plus review by exception could cut the licence fee from about a thousand dollars to about a hundred and allow continuous monitoring instead of four yearly renewal.

Source: ChinaTalk, 23 November 2026, The Agentic State

Singapore now lets civil servants vibe code their own applications against government APIs

The clearest examples of states productising AI internally are not the big announcements. Ilves described a Singapore government platform that lets civil servants build applications against standard government APIs and identity systems without engineering help, and said Estonia built a procurement tool for purchases under fifteen thousand dollars in a couple of months, staffed by procurement professionals plus one technologist, which now completes AI training procurements end to end in twenty four hours. Ukraine's Diia app, used by more than half of Ukrainian adults by 2022, added a chatbot last year and has made traffic fine payments its first transactional service.

Source: ChinaTalk, 23 November 2026, The Agentic State

Citizens with coding agents can already build better public services than the state can

Ilves' sharpest warning is that the capability gap has inverted: people with coding agents are producing better interfaces to government than government ships, and copycat versions of official portals are already being built outside it. He calls the result shadow public services, and the problem is that they raise expectations faster than the state can meet them, so satisfaction can fall even as the tooling improves. He put process re-engineering and organisational change at two thirds to three quarters of the real work in any of these projects, and proposed a shared public repository of process models, evaluations and non-personal data that governments could adapt from each other.

Source: ChinaTalk, 23 November 2026, The Agentic State

Prof G Markets calls the AI bubble popped, and says Nvidia may now be a buy

Scott Galloway and Ed Elson spent the episode on the previous week's sell off and concluded the AI bubble has finally burst. Their follow up call is that Nvidia, after losing roughly a third of its market value in a month, may now be a buying opportunity, which matches Galloway's prediction on the same day's Pivot that the stock finishes the year up. They also discussed the conditions under which active stock picking beats indexing, and gave their read on where the Anthropic listing goes next. Galloway has said he holds Anthropic only through an index fund.

Source: Prof G Markets, 23 November 2026, The AI Bubble Just Popped: Here Is What To Do

POLITICO Tech is publishing again, and its first big question is whether Anthropic's IPO broke its safety promise

The show, which had been quiet for most of the year, is back on a near daily schedule and has spent three of its last four episodes on Anthropic, including an interview with Dario Amodei on 19 November. The latest puts the obvious question to the company: now that there are public shareholders, what happens when the duty to them collides with the public good commitments the company was built on. A transcript excerpt on the episode listing carries the line that Anthropic is not living up to its original mission. The episode listing does not name the guest, so treat the attribution as unconfirmed.

Source: POLITICO Tech, 23 November 2026, Anthropic's IPO tests its safety vows

The White House is weighing a rule forcing companies to disclose use of Chinese open source models

Jason Calacanis cited Reuters reporting that the administration is considering a disclosure requirement for companies building on Chinese open weight models, with Treasury Secretary Bessent leading the hawkish side, and said the House Select Committee on China has already written to Airbnb, DoorDash and Cursor about their open model use. David Friedberg argued open weights are data rather than executables and can be audited, and compared a labelling regime to restricting Apache or Mozilla. Chamath Palihapitiya said a disclaimer would achieve little and that Washington needs more technical staff. All three invest in startups that build on open models, so the unanimity is not disinterested.

Source: All-In, 20 November 2026, Anthropic Goes Public, Nvidia in Crisis, Markets Tank, Open Source Crackdown?

Chamath says AI produces nine or ten megacorps, and the labels stop mattering

Palihapitiya's thesis is that nine to ten megacorps get built over the next decade off AI, each controlling the stack from power and data centres up to the application layer, and that investors should stop sorting companies by business model label and look at gross margin and free cash flow conversion instead. He named Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX and Meta as the ones already moving up and down that stack, and said Nvidia's era of over earning may be ending. He also argued the market now rewards acquisitions that defend a moat over buybacks, which he reads as a signal a company has run out of things to build. He said he is positioned risk off on macro, so read the structural bullishness against that.

Source: All-In, 20 November 2026, Anthropic Goes Public, Nvidia in Crisis, Markets Tank, Open Source Crackdown?
