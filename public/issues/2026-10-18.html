Meta’s Muse Spark Open puts 816 billion open weights on Apple silicon

Meta has released Muse Spark Open, an open-weight model with 816 billion total parameters, 42 billion active, and a one million token context window. Apple updated MLX so it runs on Apple silicon, but the full model wants about a terabyte of memory, roughly $40,000 of paired M5 Ultra Mac Studios, so the practical buyer is a startup that wants private inference rather than a laptop owner. It trails Kimi K3 on coding while claiming open state-of-the-art results on JobBench and MCP-Atlas, and Artificial Analysis scores it 39, seventh among open models. The episode’s argument is that this is Meta’s return to open weights after Llama 4, and that the least covered launches of the week matter most.

Microsoft has finished pre-training MAI-Base 2 at 2.8 trillion parameters

Microsoft says it has completed pre-training of MAI-Base 2, a mixture-of-experts model with 2.8 trillion total parameters and 80 billion active, trained from scratch without distillation on 16,000 GB200 chips over 38 days. Microsoft claims it beats every open-weight model it tested on held-out evaluations, and by its own private comparison outperforms the base behind GPT-5.6 Sol using roughly a quarter of the compute. The commercial point is Frontier Tuning, which would let customers fine tune their own models on that base and answer cost and sovereignty objections. On the show’s reading, a post-trained MAI-2 takes the number of US companies competing at the frontier from two to six.

Anthropic has embedded METR inside the company as a resident safety evaluator

Anthropic has given a six-person team from METR employee-level access to its systems as its first in-house safety evaluator, with SecureBio and Accenture on lower tiers and Hugging Face in early talks. METR cannot veto publication of its findings, but Anthropic can require redactions in three narrow categories: customer data, legally privileged information, and model architecture. Critics quoted in the episode question how independent this can be when METR shares backers with Anthropic, and argue the role should eventually sit with genuinely external auditors. It is the first serious attempt to put an evaluator inside a frontier lab rather than at arm’s length.

Anthropic’s pre-IPO investor day produced big numbers and mixed reactions

Anthropic presented to potential IPO investors in San Francisco ahead of a listing targeted around Thanksgiving. Figures relayed on the episode include $16.8 billion of third quarter revenue, over $5 billion a month, net revenue retention of 900 percent, an 85 percent adjusted gross margin, an 11 percent adjusted operating margin, and positive free cash flow, with circulating annualised revenue estimates ranging from $70 billion to more than $100 billion. Sacks cautioned that attendees signed non-disclosure agreements, so the leaked numbers deserve scepticism, and Horowitz said the concern is the deceleration from the second quarter to the third and the unclear annualised figure. Polymarket now puts the odds of a 2026 listing at 74 percent, down from 95 percent in early September.

CoreWeave’s credit market is now pricing default as a coin flip

Chamath Palihapitiya walked through CoreWeave’s credit default swaps at roughly 953 basis points, which implies about a 16 percent annual default probability and around 55 percent over five years. He put the company at about $35 billion of debt with another $35 billion needed to fund contracted capacity, an average funding cost near 8.5 percent, one GPU loan tranche yielding about 15 percent, and roughly $5 billion short of break even. David Sacks pushed back that each project sits in its own asset-level vehicle, so one can enter receivership without the parent failing, and noted CoreWeave is still paying its 9.6 percent unsecured coupon while the repricing hits future borrowing. All four voices here are venture investors with positions across the AI capital stack, so read the calls as interested ones.

Ben Horowitz calls neocloud finance a Rube Goldberg machine and says a16z passed

Guest hosting for David Friedberg, Ben Horowitz described CoreWeave-style businesses as “Rube Goldberg financial machines” with layered exposure to GPU contracts, data centre agreements, memory shortages and power, and said a16z stayed out because it is a dangerous arbitrage business where you can get upside down easily. He drew the comparison to his own Loudcloud years, when bandwidth fell from about $1,500 per megabit in 1999 to $50 by 2001 and borrowed money bought at the peak wiped holders out. On the other side of the ledger he noted GPU useful life has stretched from three to five years out to six, seven or eight, and cited Andy Jassy saying AWS is sold out beyond 2028. Horowitz is co-founder of a large AI and infrastructure investor, and his firm’s decision not to back neoclouds is part of the argument.

Sacks calls Anthropic’s self-replication warnings regulatory capture

In a segment the show labels Psyop Corner, David Sacks attacked Anthropic’s recent research warning that small models could soon copy themselves across home and internet-of-things devices, along with its claims about defending against distillation attacks, calling the package regulatory capture aimed at open-source models. Anthropic published the self-replication work and the distillation detection and defence papers in the middle of last week. The disagreement is not about whether the behaviour is real but about who benefits from publicising it, and the hosts making the accusation are investors in the buildout that stricter rules would slow.

Source: All-In, 17 October 2026, CoreWeave Crash, AI Trade Cracks, Anthropic IPO Red Flags, Bill Gates Doomer

The Jevons paradox has reached software pricing

Sacks argued that AI-written software is expansionary rather than merely cannibalistic, because coding assistants make bespoke software viable for niches too small to support a mass-market product, and that demand for engineers is still rising even with something like 95 percent of code now written by assistants, since someone has to manage and maintain the output. Horowitz named it the Jevons paradox of software, where unit prices fall and volume rises, and said durable moats will come from proprietary data and feedback loops rather than code. Palihapitiya predicted the pricing model itself shifts, from cost per million tokens to cost per click or cost per action, the way AdWords and AdSense reshaped online advertising.

Source: All-In, 17 October 2026, CoreWeave Crash, AI Trade Cracks, Anthropic IPO Red Flags, Bill Gates Doomer

The panel takes aim at Bill Gates as the archetypal AI doomer

The closing segment went after Gates’s warning that AI could cause a billion deaths, with Palihapitiya arguing he built quasi-governmental influence through disease eradication and the Giving Pledge, lost it after COVID, and is now reaching for a central command structure around AI. The substantive counter came from Horowitz, who cited a four-part test from Databricks chief executive Ali Ghodsi for recursive self-improvement: each new model would need far fewer GPUs, less training time, and higher accuracy, and would then have to repeat the cycle. Horowitz said none of those conditions currently holds, which is why he treats runaway improvement as theoretical for now.

The White House AI task force has started work on a 120-day safety report

The task force held its first meeting, with the agenda built around a safety report due inside 120 days and prepared with the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, covering cyber and critical infrastructure, biosecurity, and model control. A working group will implement the White House accord under which frontier labs and Nvidia commit to board-level safety oversight and external auditors, with responsibilities split across officials from Defense, the Federal Trade Commission, Justice, the Office of Personnel Management and Labor. Enforcement is the open question, because the accord is voluntary and officials have floated export controls as the only real penalty.

Europe told the Dublin AI summit it does not need new AI rules

At the International AI Summit in Dublin, EU officials said no further rules are needed while Europe closes the gap with the US technology sector, which inverts the usual positions: American labs have been pressing governments for rules that would slow advanced models, and the regulator is the one asking for room to run. The episode questions whether the risk-based approach in the AI Act can reach problems that originate inside leading US labs at all. China’s stock of DUV immersion machines has roughly doubled since the 2023 controls

Saif Khan, founder of the Center for Technology and Statecraft and previously at Commerce and the National Security Council, says a new report led by Nick Brown finds about 170 to 180 deep ultraviolet immersion lithography tools were inside China when the 2023 US controls took effect, and that the stockpile has roughly doubled since, because less advanced immersion tools are still permitted and tools can be diverted even when named facilities are blocked. Only Japan and the Netherlands make these machines, and Huawei uses DUV to produce its most advanced chips at the 7 nanometre node. Khan calls DUV immersion the single most consequential lever on the US and China AI balance into the 2030s.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 October 2026, Saif Khan: The Case for DUV Controls

The MATCH Act is now the vehicle for any DUV export controls

Khan ties the analysis to the MATCH Act, S. 4281 in the current Congress, which covers semiconductor equipment exports to China and could carry controls on immersion lithography tools. He notes the 2025 AI Action Plan named closing loopholes in equipment controls as a priority and that the administration has not rolled back existing ones, and argues every month without new controls erodes leverage, though rules adopted a year or two out would still matter. The report’s modelling projects US output near one billion H100 equivalents a year by the mid-2030s against a ceiling near 10 million for China if DUV exports stopped now, with the ranges overlapping if they continue. These are a think tank’s projections made in support of a specific policy outcome, with wide error bars acknowledged.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 October 2026, Saif Khan: The Case for DUV Controls

Robotics is named the next front, and the policy work itself was written with coding agents

Khan frames robotics as the next nexus of US and China technology competition and says the Federal Communications Commission has taken steps toward an import ban on certain Chinese robots, a claim worth confirming independently given the unusual remit. He also points to a paper setting out 23 low-regret policy options for a world of increasingly automated AI research, while arguing some situations will need bolder moves. The incidental detail is the method: Khan says the DUV analysis was produced by a small team using Claude Code and Codex alongside purchased datasets, which makes this one of the clearer examples of coding agents being used inside national security policy research.

Barclays expects a Tesla and SpaceX merger before December

Analyst Dan Levy suggests an all-stock deal at roughly 0.7 to 1, which would leave Elon Musk with about 37 percent ownership and 56 percent of the voting power, with the joint Terafab chip plant as the main reason to do it. Nothing has surfaced to corroborate it: no bankers, no board committee, no filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and prediction markets put the odds at 24 percent. It is an analyst thesis rather than a deal, but a merger on those terms would fold the chip supply chain of both companies into one balance sheet.
