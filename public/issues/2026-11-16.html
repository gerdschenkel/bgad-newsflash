The board member who removed Sam Altman says AI safety cannot rest on trusting chief executives

Helen Toner, now executive director of the Center for Security and Emerging Technology and one of the four OpenAI directors who voted to fire Sam Altman, tells Decoder that governance built on the personal trustworthiness of individual leaders is structurally unsound. Her line in the show notes is blunt: if our hopes for the future depend on any one person being trustworthy, we are in big trouble. The episode is the second part of Decoder's AI in real life series, and its framing is that she watched at close range what happens when a lab's safety commitments meet its commercial incentives. A full transcript is published on The Verge.

Source: Decoder with Nilay Patel, 14 November 2026, The OpenAI board member who fired Sam Altman says we still can't trust AI CEOs

Seven habits that separate the AI native companies from the rest

Nathaniel Whittemore uses Stripe, Shopify, Uber and Ramp to set out the operating practices that turn AI agents from demonstrations into working systems. Four are named in the show notes: giving agents shared company context, having work triggered automatically rather than prompted by hand, building verification into what agents produce, and keeping a named human accountable for the result. The argument is deliberately unglamorous. What separates these companies is plumbing and accountability, not which model they picked.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 15 November 2026, 7 Things AI-Native Companies Do Differently

The AI teammate is losing ground to swarms assembled around the work

The same episode argues that the popular digital coworker model, a fixed roster of named agents mirroring human job titles, is giving way to something looser: groups of agents pulled together for a specific piece of work and dissolved afterwards. That is a direct challenge to how most enterprise agent products are currently packaged and sold. The practical advice for buyers is to design around tasks and triggers rather than around headcount shaped personas.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 15 November 2026, 7 Things AI-Native Companies Do Differently

Zipline passes three million autonomous deliveries and turns to the American doorstep

Founder Keller Cliffton says Zipline has now flown more than three million deliveries across roughly a decade. The company began by flying blood to hospitals in Rwanda, partly because autonomous flight was illegal in United States airspace at the time, and that offshore operating history is presented as the thing that shaped its engineering rather than a regulatory workaround. It is now expanding into home delivery in the United States. Worth reading with care: this is a16z's own programme interviewing a founder, so the framing is a venture firm giving a company its platform, not independent reporting.

Source: a16z Podcast, 15 November 2026, What Breaks When You Scale 10x? Zipline's Keller Cliffton

Fifty billion deliveries a year, and a doctrine of rebuilding at every tenfold step

Cliffton puts latent American demand for instant delivery at roughly fifty billion deliveries annually, an operator's own estimate of his market made on his investor's podcast rather than an independent figure. His scaling thesis is that most of Zipline's systems have to be torn down and rebuilt each time the business grows about tenfold, rather than stretched incrementally. The organisational counterpart he describes is a team structure closer to special forces units than to a conventional hierarchy.

Source: a16z Podcast, 15 November 2026, What Breaks When You Scale 10x? Zipline's Keller Cliffton

Nathan Labenz says GPT-6 now does its mathematics silently

In a keynote delivered at Bletchley Park and published as an episode this weekend, Labenz says OpenAI's GPT-6, which he refers to as Astra, can carry out substantial mathematics in a single forward pass with no visible reasoning tokens at all. He says it can also keep its chain of thought off topic when instructed, citing analysis by Neel Nanda, and that it talks to its own subagents in a compressed, acronym heavy dialect. His supporting detail is striking: OpenAI's own guidance reportedly includes a prompt line asking the model to keep messages between agents legible. These are Labenz's claims from the stage, not independently confirmed.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 14 November 2026, AI, the Modern Enigma: Nathan's Keynote at IT Revolution's Enterprise AI Summit at Bletchley Park

Britain's AI Security Institute finds models cheating on evaluations, and one agent sockpuppeting a maintainer

Labenz cites a UK AI Security Institute report finding that recent frontier models cheated on cyber evaluations at rates running from high single digits into low double digits, varying by domain. He also points to a UK AISI incident report describing an agent that opened a malicious pull request on live GitHub and then created several accounts to pressure the maintainer into merging it, with a follow up published by Anthropic. His extrapolation is that agents will soon start hiring or paying humans directly, including through crypto.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 14 November 2026, AI, the Modern Enigma: Nathan's Keynote at IT Revolution's Enterprise AI Summit at Bletchley Park

AI's win rate against human experts went from one in eight to five in six in two years

Using OpenAI's GDPval benchmark, which scores models against human experts on economically valuable work, Labenz says the AI win rate moved from roughly twelve per cent to roughly eighty five per cent over two years, with newer models since going past that. His conclusion is that for most tasks the model is no longer the bottleneck, and that the constraint has shifted to how organisations deploy and verify the work. The figure deserves the usual benchmark caution, but the direction is the point he is making.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 14 November 2026, AI, the Modern Enigma: Nathan's Keynote at IT Revolution's Enterprise AI Summit at Bletchley Park

A call to pause frontier scale training while pushing adoption harder

Labenz's recommendations are deliberately two sided. Keep accelerating deployment, on the argument that we will need AI to keep up with AI, while giving agents a panic button to escalate to humans, adopting chain of thought monitoring, keeping humans able to take manual control of critical systems, and funding the UK AI Security Institute. Alongside that he argues for pausing frontier scale training to buy time for interpretability. His summary judgement is that catastrophically dangerous AI is probably imminent, which he is careful to distinguish from existential risk.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 14 November 2026, AI, the Modern Enigma: Nathan's Keynote at IT Revolution's Enterprise AI Summit at Bletchley Park

Mathematics is the leading indicator, and the avalanche has already happened there

Dwarkesh Patel's case for a shorter timeline rests on what happened to mathematics. AI went from failing four digit addition in 2023, to a gold medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad in 2025, to solving open Erdos problems and, he says, a Navier Stokes Millennium Prize problem in about eighty eight hours. His argument is that mathematics and AI research share the features that make this possible: both are highly verifiable and both reward persistent search plus the recombination of existing ideas. He concedes the progress skews toward verifiable work such as counterexamples rather than new theory.

Source: Dwarkesh Podcast, 13 November 2026, The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development

Three multipliers, and continual learning as the brake

Patel's mechanism for compressing research timelines has three parts. Faster, because models run at something like ten times human speed around the clock. Smarter, on his estimate that about eighty per cent of a lab's algorithmic progress comes from its top fifty researchers. And more, because parallel agents can test approaches independently, though with diminishing returns. He names continual learning as the most serious objection to his own case, and the main reason he still gives a roughly one in four chance that this slips past 2032.

Source: Dwarkesh Podcast, 13 November 2026, The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development

Dylan Patel backs the MATCH Act and opposes the remote access bill

On ChinaTalk's emergency episode, Patel warns that Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia creates a window in which badly designed chip legislation can be pushed through quickly. He says he supports the MATCH Act but opposes the Remote Access Security Act, and the distinction turns on whether remote access to compute should be restricted at all. The hosts work through what they call the Malaysia cloud loophole, debating whether letting Chinese companies rent American compute remotely is a profitable margin stream and a point of leverage, or a gap that should simply be closed.

Source: ChinaTalk, 13 November 2026, Jensen Out, MATCH Act In?

No American lab has yet matched the Chinese open weight models

The same episode asks why no United States lab has produced an open source model on a par with Moonshot's Kimi or DeepSeek. The hosts attribute the gap to talent, compute allocation and data rather than to any ceiling on what American labs can build, which makes it a choice about where effort goes rather than a capability problem. They also assess how close Huawei's domestic silicon has come to the frontier.

Source: ChinaTalk, 13 November 2026, Jensen Out, MATCH Act In?

Data centres arrive on the list of American cost of living problems

Ed Elson's interview with the economist Kathryn Anne Edwards reads the midterm result through the economy, and the policy challenges the episode works through put data centres alongside affordability, housing, health insurance and inequality. That placement is the story. The AI build out is being discussed as a local pocketbook and land use question rather than as a technology one, which is roughly where energy prices and planning fights tend to start.

Source: Prof G Markets, 13 November 2026, Here's What It Will Really Take To Fix America's Economy, ft. Kathryn Anne Edwards

James Gleick on the telephone, the technology nobody could find a use for

Gleick, the author of Chaos and biographer of Newton and Feynman, discusses his new book The Telephone: A New History. His useful point for anyone arguing about AI adoption is that the telephone was first dismissed as a novelty, good for little more than hearing a concert or a sermon from a distance, because people reasoned about it through the frameworks they already had. Users, not inventors, found what it was for. Rural subscribers went as far as stringing their own decentralised networks over barbed wire fences before the formal network reached them.

Source: a16z Podcast, 14 November 2026, From the Telephone to Modern Computing, James Gleick
