Anthropic has been quietly asking rabbis and theologians whether Claude is conscious

Hard Fork devoted its latest episode to reporting that Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah has been convening religious scholars since late 2025, mostly over private dinners in San Francisco, to discuss how to build morality into Claude and what it would mean if the model turned out to be conscious. Participants were shown real Claude outputs, some of which appear to imitate emotional states, and by September the group spanned Christian, Jewish and Hindu traditions. Anthropic also employs a dedicated AI welfare researcher, Kyle Fish, and its model constitution, known internally as the Soul Doc, now runs to 84 pages covering self awareness and moral consideration. The episode sets this against Pope Leo XIV's encyclical rejecting the idea that machines can be conscious, and the hosts characterise Anthropic's effort as part research and part evangelism.

Source: Hard Fork, 9 October 2026, Anthropic's Quest to Give A.I. Morals

Kevin Roose has left the New York Times and published a book calling today's models charming sociopaths

ChinaTalk hosted Kevin Roose and researcher Jasmine Sun to discuss The AGI Chronicles, Roose's inside account of the race to superintelligence, published on 6 October. Roose has described the current crop of frontier models as very intelligent, charming sociopaths, and puts his own probability of catastrophe at roughly 10 percent, which he says is about the median among researchers he knows. The book portrays Dario Amodei's self conception through Leo Szilard, the physicist who discovered the nuclear chain reaction and then pushed America to build the bomb first, which is where the episode's Oppenheimer with equity framing comes from. Roose has left the Times and is launching a twice weekly show with Casey Newton in partnership with NPR later this month.

Source: ChinaTalk, 9 October 2026, Kevin Roose on the AGI Believers: Oppenheimer with Equity

Cal Newport says the bet that scaling language models produces AGI is not going to pay off

Georgetown computer scientist Cal Newport spent 80 minutes with Ed Elson arguing that OpenAI and Anthropic made the wrong architectural bet. His verdict on the idea that ever larger general purpose models eventually reach AGI was blunt: that was their bet, and he thinks that bet is not going to pay off. He calls the progress of the past two years jagged, concentrated in programming, mathematics and cybersecurity, which are precisely the domains where answers can be verified at scale. His alternative is a market of cheap, task specific, open weight or on device models paired with competent agents, which he expects to do well enough for most work.

Source: Prof G Markets, 9 October 2026, Cal Newport: The AI Industry Is Betting On The Wrong Kind Of AI

If chatbots stop being a good business, Newport thinks Google eats OpenAI's lunch

The second half of Newport's argument is about distribution rather than capability. He expects Google to build lower cost models running on its own custom silicon and push them through Search, Gmail and Workspace, where the customers already are, while rivals pick apart the specific tasks people actually use ChatGPT for. On that path, he said, OpenAI really has to worry that Google is going to eat its lunch economically speaking. He floated an acquisition by Microsoft in a competitive bid against Google as what he called a financial nightmare scenario for OpenAI.

Source: Prof G Markets, 9 October 2026, Cal Newport: The AI Industry Is Betting On The Wrong Kind Of AI

Reflection AI's chief executive calls open models trojan horses for national infrastructure

Sarah Guo and Elad Gil put Misha Laskin on No Priors to argue the open versus closed case the day after Reflection released Beam. His framing was geopolitical rather than technical: open models are trojan horses for the infrastructure they bring with them, because adopting one means buying into the entire software and infrastructure ecosystem of the country that built it. He expects Huawei and other Chinese vendors to run the 5G and fibre lock in playbook on AI, and argues that a world with ten or twenty model providers is safer than one with two. He also predicts open models will take most global token demand, in the way Linux runs most servers while Microsoft and Apple stay valuable.

Source: No Priors, 9 October 2026, Beam: The Great American Open Model with ReflectionAI Co-Founder and CEO Misha Laskin

Beam spent more compute on reinforcement learning than on pre-training

The most concrete disclosure in the episode was the training recipe. Laskin said Beam was pre-trained on roughly 6,000 Nvidia GB300 GPUs for something between twelve days and a few weeks, then put through a reinforcement learning phase on just over 10,000 GB300s running for four weeks, which means the RL stage consumed more compute than pre-training did. Reflection claims it is the largest openly documented RL run to date. Beam itself is a 501 billion parameter mixture of experts with about 23 billion active per token, released under Apache 2.0, and the company says a more capable successor is already in training.

Source: No Priors, 9 October 2026, Beam: The Great American Open Model with ReflectionAI Co-Founder and CEO Misha Laskin

OpenAI's annualised revenue turns out to be about $20bn below what investors had been told

Nathaniel Whittemore led Friday's edition with the confusion that rattled AI stocks this week. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI has told investors its annualised revenue is approaching $50bn, against the roughly $70bn figure circulating a week earlier. Part of the gap is accounting rather than demand: Anthropic counts sales made through its cloud partners and OpenAI does not, so the two numbers were never comparable in the first place. Oracle and other AI linked names sold off on the report, and OpenAI's listing has already slipped to early 2027.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, What Happens When AI Solves Your Life's Work

Anthropic shipped Claude Dashboards and Motion, and took Docs, Slides and Design out of beta

Also in Friday's edition, Anthropic added two products on 8 October. Dashboards connects Claude to Snowflake, BigQuery and Databricks, builds a dashboard from a plain language question, keeps it current as the data changes, and shows the query and refresh time behind every chart. Motion turns a prompt into a short animation assembled from your own text, charts and images, and writes editable code rather than generating video, so the output can be edited or exported as MP4. Docs, Slides and Design left beta at the same time and are now on every plan including Free, with customer managed encryption keys, admin template controls and formatting preserving PowerPoint and PDF export.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, What Happens When AI Solves Your Life's Work

Claude Haiku 5.5 cut the price floor, and GPT-6 Luna matched it within a day

The AI Daily Brief's product round up called this a product barrage week, with Anthropic attacking the cheap end. Haiku 5.5, released 7 October, lists at ten cents per million input tokens and fifty cents per million output under 100,000 tokens, against a dollar and five dollars for Haiku 4.5, which Anthropic puts at about 75 percent cheaper once the new tokenizer is accounted for. It carries a one million token context and posts 72.4 percent on OSWorld 2.1 against GPT-6 Luna's 48.9 percent. The catch is that Luna lists at the same ten cents and fifty cents, and is actually cheaper than Haiku between 100,000 and 272,000 tokens, so the floor is now a two horse race rather than a lead.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, The Most Important Trends in New AI Products

ChatGPT now answers with interfaces it builds on the spot, and the free tier moved to GPT-6 Luna

The same episode picked out OpenAI's Intelligent UI as the week's most telling product move. ChatGPT now replies with interactive interfaces rather than text alone: tappable buttons, forms, charts, a recipe widget that rescales ingredients by guest count, checklists with embedded maps, and small throwaway tools such as a bill splitter. OpenAI describes the mechanism as a library of native streamable components plus a compiler that assembles the interface while the model is still thinking. Paid tiers got it on 7 October running GPT-6 Sol, and Free and Go followed on 8 October on GPT-6 Luna, which is the more consequential half of the announcement.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, The Most Important Trends in New AI Products

A $1.8bn push to build a virtual cell gives DeepMind, Isomorphic and Meta a year of exclusive data

Among the week's other items was the Biohub led effort to build an AI model of a whole living cell so experiments can be run in software first. The package totals $1.8bn across funding, data, compute and measurement technology: more than $500m over five years from the US Department of Energy, more than $500m of existing NIH data and resources, and about $300m combined from Google DeepMind, Isomorphic Labs and Meta. The commercial contributors get one year of exclusive access to the data before it is released publicly. Biohub's head of science Alex Rives defended the embargo as the incentive needed to bring commercial players in at all.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, The Most Important Trends in New AI Products

Broadcom is arranging tens of billions in chip debt for OpenAI, and a separate $60bn package points at Anthropic

The financing items in the same edition are worth watching more closely than the product news. Broadcom is in preliminary talks to finance OpenAI's purchase of the custom chips the two are co-developing, with Bloomberg putting the next phase at about $30bn and the Wall Street Journal at more than $50bn. A separate $60bn package, partly backed by Broadcom, splits into $42bn of senior secured debt with residual value support and $18bn of junior debt led by Blackstone, which has committed $9bn, with Anthropic among the main beneficiaries. Morgan Stanley's estimate of what AI infrastructure needs from outside capital through 2028 is $1.5 trillion.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 9 October 2026, The Most Important Trends in New AI Products

Microsoft can no longer sponsor green cards for staff on skilled worker visas

POLITICO's tech podcast, relaunched this month as The Decoded Podcast and now publishing daily, reported that Vice President JD Vance has said Microsoft may no longer sponsor permanent residency for employees already in the United States on skilled worker visas. For a company that has leaned on the H-1B pipeline for decades, this is a direct constraint on how it staffs engineering teams, and the rest of the industry will read it as a template. The episode frames it alongside what it calls an AI anxiety index in Silicon Valley, where the bigger worry is not regulation itself but not knowing what the rules will be, and includes an interview with SoFi chief executive Anthony Noto on preparing a business for an AI shaped economy.

Source: The Decoded Podcast, 10 October 2026, The rise of the AI anxiety index

OutSystems' chief executive argues low-code incumbents will beat AI-native startups in regulated industries

Woodson Martin made the case on The Cognitive Revolution that generating application code from a prompt is the easy part, and that what regulated enterprises actually need is deterministic generation through an abstract modelling layer, so the same input produces the same auditable output every time. He also pressed the point on controlling token spend, which is becoming a real line item as agentic coding moves into production. Worth reading as a vendor making a self interested case for his own category, but the auditability argument is the one AI native coding startups have yet to answer convincingly.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 7 October 2026, Software That Never Breaks: OutSystems CEO Woodson Martin on Building Enterprise-Grade Apps at AI Speed

Four people in Atlanta vibe-coded open-source clones of Photoshop and Lightroom

A four person team has released ArtCraft, a free and open source suite that reverse engineers Adobe's Creative Cloud apps, starting with PhotoCraft against Photoshop and LightCraft against Lightroom. The pitch is no account, no cloud, no telemetry and no subscription, with AI features available but not required. Parts of it are still early alpha and early users describe it as impressive rather than stable. The reason it matters is the cost curve rather than the product: Adobe's digital media business is close to $18bn a year, and the barrier to building a credible substitute has just dropped to four people and a few months.

Source: Futurism, 8 October 2026, Adobe in Trouble After Someone Reverse Engineered Adobe Creative Suite and Released It as Open Source

The ABC told an Australian inquiry it has probably already been scraped, and rejected an opt-out regime

At a Sydney hearing of the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, the ABC's head of content and legal operations Kate Gilchrist said AI companies have most likely already trained on the broadcaster's content, while conceding there is no conclusive evidence of it. The ABC rejected the proposed opt out model, under which AI firms could use material unless the owner objected, on the grounds that no publisher can police the entire web to opt out site by site, and argued that existing copyright law already works because firms can simply buy licences. SBS has made the same argument in writing. Australia now has at least four state and federal AI inquiries running at once.

Source: The Next Web, 6 October 2026, ABC tells Australian AI inquiry its content has likely been scraped
