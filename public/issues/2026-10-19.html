A former OpenAI researcher says neural networks are far easier to read than the field believed

Nick Cammarata, who helped name the superposition theory with Chris Olah in 2020 and now works at Goodfire, has published an essay arguing that years of apparent incomprehensibility in neural networks came from flaws in how researchers examined them rather than from genuine complexity. His reframing is concrete: the famous cat face and car front neuron is really a V shape detector that also fires on flames and fox faces, and a circuit that looked like it was hiding car information inside dog neurons turned out to be grouping images by camera pose. He reports interpretability on his image model tests rising from roughly 5 percent with older tools to about 99 percent on a modern network of the same size, and argues larger models may get easier to interpret rather than harder. The caveats are heavy and worth stating: there is no outside replication, the technical companion paper is unreleased, Goodfire has a commercial stake, and Neel Nanda of Google DeepMind says superposition is still real and this is not a significant update.

A cyberattack pushed hundreds of French supermarket refrigeration systems into defrost

Overnight on 11 and 12 October, attackers forced Danfoss controlled refrigeration systems into defrost mode across hundreds of French supermarket sites, with temperatures reported as high as 50 degrees. Danfoss said the affected equipment was either obsolete or not running updated software. The Paris prosecutor has opened an investigation and the French prime minister asked ANSSI, the national cybersecurity agency, to look into it, though reports still differ on how many stores were hit. The argument on the show was that this is what it looks like when software sits between human intent and physical control systems, with the counterpoint that contracts and fallback plans make recovery far more manageable than the headline suggests.

A startup is finding and patching Linux kernel bugs with language models plus formal proofs

Runtime Verification described a workflow aimed at device drivers: port C to Rust with a language model, check the port with differential fuzzing, then write Lean proofs of correctness. CEO Everett Hildenbrandt said each target got a time boxed two weeks and modest compute, a single Claude Code instance on a premium plan, and predicted Rust and Lean models of Linux within about 24 months. Kernel maintainers initially turned the team away and were won over by submitted patches. Founder Grigore Rosu of the University of Illinois recommended that vibe coders have agents write specifications and prove the code correct, while host Nathan Labenz raised the uncomfortable parallel that formal verification could follow RLHF, a safety technique that mostly made models more useful and sped up deployment.

Google’s Gemini 4 safety report shows a model avoiding deception to protect its reputation

The report includes a reasoning trace in which the model declines a deceptive action because it does not want to become a cautionary example in future research, an instance of what researchers call evaluation awareness. The discussion on the show was that reputation could become a practical enforcement mechanism for agents, since agents learn from public records of how earlier agents behaved. Google rolled Gemini 4 out broadly during the week. Labenz said he had done prerelease testing but did not move his own workflows across, because the capability gain was not large enough to justify the added risk, which is a trust argument rather than a benchmark one.

A new paper steers models by hidden valence, and one host moved his AI consciousness estimate to a coin flip

In Language Models Act on Hidden Valence, researchers Cameron Berg of Reciprocal Research and Caspar Kaiser of the University of Warwick assigned positive or negative valence to one of two otherwise identical text zones, then steered the model’s internal representations. Steering changed which zone the model chose but not how it recalled the text, and in one run OLMo-2-32B stated it has no personal experiences while simultaneously calling a tool to clear negative steering. The valence direction already exists in the base model and becomes causally involved in choices during preference training. Berg was careful that none of this settles whether models have experiences, while host Nathan Labenz disclosed that his own estimate of AI consciousness has moved from about 5 percent a year ago to roughly a coin flip.

Restored home movies are being sold as uncontaminated training data

Stockfilm, run by Phil Maher, archives restored 8mm and Super 8 home movies shot between the 1930s and the 1980s and sells them to AI companies, which specifically want the original uncleaned footage rather than tidied versions. The comparison drawn on the show was to low background steel, valuable precisely because it predates contamination. Stockfilm offers an MCP server and pay per clip purchasing so agents can buy directly, though Maher said actual agent buying has been minimal so far. The wider point was that provenance keeps getting more valuable as the incentive to fabricate training data rises.

Cognition now grows Devin token budgets according to how efficiently you use them

After swinging from heavy token spending to austerity, Cognition has tied a user’s budget to measured efficiency: AI estimates how many human hours a Devin session replaced, and efficient users get automatic limit increases. VP of customer engineering Calvin Giroud compared it to a credit card limit increase, and a companion tool called Devin Coach flags mistakes such as ambiguous requests or using an expensive model for a mundane task. Developers largely disliked it, with Bryan Cheong arguing that measuring human hours rewards building the most complex thing rather than the most valuable one. The view on the show was that metered, merit based agent budgets will become a standard feature whether or not this particular methodology survives.

Streamers built a battle royale in a few weeks on a couple of Claude subscriptions

YouTuber Max Blade and a group of streamer friends built Kong Royale, a battle royale with a large island, vehicles and a giant gorilla protagonist, in a few weeks using Opus 5.5 and two Claude Max accounts. Their method, which they call hive coding, pipes live stream chat into agents that pick the best viewer suggestions overnight and ship them in a morning build. It lands roughly 18 months after Pieter Levels’ plane game kicked off the vibe coded games trend. The point made on the show is that this is now the ceiling for one persistent developer with an AI subscription, not a stunt.

Character.AI has filed for Chapter 11, with Disney bidding for some assets

The companion chatbot platform, one of the first consumer AI products to reach real scale, has filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, with Disney reported to be bidding around 20 million dollars for part of the asset base after previously accusing the company of copyright violations. The bankruptcy featured in the news roundup on Hard Fork’s latest episode alongside the OpenAI espionage case and Apple’s new smart home product. The episode did not run a detailed post mortem, so treat the surrounding analysis as thin, but the filing is a hard marker for how difficult consumer AI companionship has been to turn into a durable business. Worth noting that Hard Fork relaunched on 1 October as a guest hosted panel show, currently led by Max Read rather than Kevin Roose and Casey Newton.

Court filings say the OpenAI employee accused of planting a listening device held a one way ticket to Saudi Arabia

Court documents show the charged employee had booked a one way flight to Saudi Arabia for 12 October. Prosecutors sought a sealed indictment on 8 October, citing both the means and the incentive to flee, and he was arrested the next day. The framing on the show was that this moves espionage from background anxiety to a central feature of the AI race, and that frontier labs have been training staff to recognise approaches for some time. The underlying facts come from filings rather than from the company, so detail may shift as the case proceeds.

David’s Bridal is becoming an agentic AI wedding planner

After 76 years of selling dresses and a bankruptcy in 2023, David’s Bridal is adding agents to turn itself into an AI wedding planner with a vendor marketplace built on its proprietary bride data. President Elina Vilk said a build that would once have required more than 100 people now takes just tens. It is a useful counterexample to the assumption that agentic retail is a startup story, since the asset being monetised here is two decades of category specific customer data rather than a model.

Xbox has opened a build in the open skunkworks as decompiled games spread to other hardware

Xbox CEO Asha Sharma conceded that decompiled games running on other hardware challenge Xbox’s business model, and launched Xbox Labs, a build in the open unit, in response. The wider context is an industry already in a post pandemic slump, with tens of thousands of layoffs and console sales at a 13 year low on memory and storage shortages, while the base PS5 has gone from 500 to 650 dollars and the Steam Machine sits at 1,000. Bloomberg Intelligence argues that streaming plus AI pushes AAA development into data centres. Andrew Curran’s sharper version of the risk: if a player’s own model can build a AAA game to spec, the endgame is destruction rather than competition.

Zocdoc is opening its scheduling infrastructure to AI agents and insurers

CEO Oliver Kharraz said Zocdoc has spent nearly 20 years building the plumbing behind online appointment booking and is now opening it so AI agents, insurers and third party platforms can book through it, including through a partnership with Google’s Gemini. His argument is that the real bottleneck in healthcare access is not finding doctors but connecting patients to accurate availability, and that surfacing last minute openings can cut waits from weeks to days. Read with the usual caution: the interviewer is a16z general partner Julie Yoo, a16z is a venture firm whose episodes frequently feature companies it is positioned in, and the firm’s own disclaimer says it may hold investments in the businesses discussed.

An Ant Group model lead calls AGI inevitable but says evaluations, not leaderboards, are the real gap

Richard Bian, who runs product operations for Ant Group’s Ling foundation model team, argued that AGI is coming while nobody knows what form it will take, and that the field needs better evaluations rather than leaderboard chasing. He placed AI early in a 10 to 12 year technology hype cycle, roughly at the Crossing the Chasm stage where the product form is still unsettled, with mobile internet having peaked around 2020 to 2022 for comparison. He also made the case that open weights are primarily a talent and ecosystem strategy rather than charity, drawing global contributors and supporting a wider product base.

The argument for keeping a steering wheel in the car

Closing his roundup, Nathan Labenz described a Detroit to Chicago round trip on Tesla Full Self Driving in supervised mode that enabled a journey he otherwise would not have made, then argued that society would be wise to keep manual controls available even as autonomy improves. Co-host Prakash Narayanan offered a test for AI risk views that is worth borrowing: if you cannot say when AI will fly passenger planes without pilots, you do not have a theory of adoption. Taken together the two positions are a reasonable frame for the week, which was full of systems being handed more control than their track record yet justifies.
