A 27 billion parameter model is beating frontier models by directing them

London lab Inherent Laboratories has published Faraday, a 27 billion parameter agent post-trained specifically for research work. The lab reports it beat Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 at replicating scientific papers, while using GPT-5.5 Codex as a tool to do the implementation. Co-founder Louis Kirsch describes the design as separating the scientist from the coder, with a small model driving a much larger one. If it holds up, the interesting unit of capability stops being parameter count and becomes who is giving the instructions.

Arm has built its own silicon for the first time in 35 years, and calls it an AGI CPU

Mohamed Awad, EVP of Cloud AI at Arm, discussed the Arm AGI CPU, co-developed with Meta and pitched around the premise that agents do not sleep. The design case is the CPU as coordinator inside an agentic system, with attention on bandwidth per core and milliwatt level power. Nathan Labenz pushed back on the framing afterwards, noting the CPU is not deciding anything, the model emits tokens that execute as real commands, and that is where tentacles reach out into the wider internet.

Google bought Spirit Airlines' data out of bankruptcy

Eon co-founders Ofir Ehrlich and Gonen Stein open on that transaction as the clearest signal yet that the industry has repriced real world enterprise datasets. Their argument is that compute and models attract the attention while historical enterprise data, the kind sitting untouched in backups, is now what AI labs are paying for access to. The blocker, they say, is not storage but discovery and permissioning, because the data is scattered and locked across business units.

Ransomware defences assume the attacker has no credentials, and agents do

The sharpest claim in the same episode is a security one. Traditional ransomware protection is built on the assumption that an attacker is operating without legitimate access, whereas an autonomous agent running with proper system permissions can do comparable damage while looking entirely authorised. Ehrlich and Stein frame the influx of autonomous agents and non-human identities as a genuinely new attack surface that existing tooling was not designed to see.

The FINRA for AI executive order has stalled

A drafted executive order would have created an industry self-regulatory body for AI modelled on FINRA, requiring labs to share security notes, safety test new models and write industry rules. Demis Hassabis proposed the idea in July and Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly backed it. Reported opposition from David Sacks appears to have helped stall it. Sacks called it a horrible idea and a Trojan horse for an open source model ban, arguing frontier labs would push standards that open model developers could not meet.

Salesforce and Anthropic launched Claude Force, and the labs are going through software vendors rather than around them

A set of plugins and skills now lets Claude agents work a Salesforce CRM directly, using Salesforce data as context, with agentic actions respecting existing governance guardrails. Marc Benioff's line was that the UI is the AI. The read on the AI Daily Brief is that a frontier lab routing through an incumbent, rather than trying to replace it, signals that data, embedded workflows and incumbency are still real moats. On All-In, David Sacks made the same point to founders: build the best agent interface, because your future users are agents rather than humans clicking buttons.

Chamath's third phase of AI is contextualisation, and it favours the systems of record

Chamath Palihapitiya laid out three phases: models as the brain, harnesses as the eyes, hands and memory, and now contextualisation, where an agent has to be trained into a specific job using proprietary data, workflows and systems of record. His conclusion is that whoever holds the context makes agents useful, so incumbents like Salesforce become more important rather than less. He also predicted the clean separation between chip supplier, hyperscaler, model lab and application company melts within five years, with every large AI company running its own cloud, models, silicon and data centres. Note that he had publicly argued Salesforce was oversold and presented the week's results as vindication, so this is a position call as much as an analysis.

Grok Voice is resolving 15,000 Starlink support calls a day

Grok Voice Think Fast 2.0 now tops the Artificial Analysis speech to speech index, which measures whether a voice agent can reason over what it hears and finish tool using tasks rather than just sound fluent. At Starlink it diagnoses hardware issues, ships replacements and fulfils more than 3,000 orders a week across voice and chat. This is one of the first properly large scale numbers on voice agents doing transactional work rather than deflecting tickets.

Ethan Mollick's verdict was that a line in AI video was crossed. Investor Steve Jang reads it as the opening for fully generated, massively multiplayer live visual worlds.

Offensive security capability is already uneven across models, and the gap closes in about six months

Malte Ubl, CTO of Vercel, runs the AI Gateway and therefore sees traffic across every major provider. He said Kimi K3 already has offensive security capability with no meaningful guardrail, while Fable 5 refuses both halves of defensive security work and Sol 5.6 and Opus 5 will do both. His estimate: in six months at the latest there will be Fable class models that do offensive security. The practical point for security teams is that model choice, not model generation, currently determines what an attacker can buy.

Photonic chips are being made on 90 nanometre European fabs, two decades behind the frontier

Michael Foertsch, founder of Q.ANT, a TRUMPF spin-out in Stuttgart, explained photonic computing as trading data complexity for function complexity, citing the estimate that roughly 95 per cent of a conventional chip's energy goes to moving data rather than computing it. Q.ANT fabricates on 90 nanometre lines, and existing European fabs agreed to convert those lines. Prakash Narayanan's counter on the show was that silicon improves roughly fourfold a year, so a new architecture has to beat where the incumbent will be, not where it is.

A humanoid robot ran the 100 metres in 8.64 seconds in Beijing

The World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing drew 2,056 robots from 666 teams across 51 events, and Tiangong Ultra set a Games record of 8.64 seconds over 100 metres, faster than Usain Bolt's human record of 9.58. David Sacks used the event to argue that the gap between the United States and China on AI is now mostly a gap in optimism rather than capability. Jason Calacanis noted that China filled a stadium with people cheering for robots while the American conversation runs on job losses and data centre siting.

The row over Druckenmiller's AI assisted op-ed splits the table

Criticism that Stanley Druckenmiller used AI to help write his Wall Street Journal op-ed set off the most useful disagreement of the week. Chamath and Sacks defended him, with Sacks saying you have to be pretty dumb these days not to use AI to help you write, and disclosing that he uses it to fact check and line edit. David Friedberg compared it to Photoshop, synthesisers and spreadsheets, arguing that used AI is too vague a category to mean anything. Calacanis dissented, calling undisclosed AI drafting the lip syncing of writing, on the grounds that readers are promised an author's own voice.
