Anthropic's costs are exploding, and the number to watch is its credit rating

Ed Elson went through Anthropic's amended S-1 with John Foley, who runs the Financial Times Lex column, ahead of an IPO expected later this month. Foley's argument is that the revenue line matters less than the company's likely credit rating, because the enterprise business that funds it is growing fast but is finite in size. The structural problem he identifies is that Google and Meta can bankroll AI losses out of enormous existing advertising businesses while Anthropic and OpenAI cannot. The question the segment ends on is whether AI companies will eventually have to degrade service because they cannot afford the tokens.

Source: Prof G Markets, 3 November 2026, Anthropic's Q3 Results Are In, The Costs Are Exploding

Anthropic's own filing says self-improving AI may be imminent

Buried in the same amended filing is a warning that Anthropic's internal estimates indicate it may imminently meet its Responsible Scaling Policy definition of dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI. The filing concedes that the company has in the past failed to identify a significant acceleration until after it happened, and says no current model has been designated as crossing the threshold. Anthropic adds that it does not expect to be able to safely let Claude design and build successive frontier generations without meaningful human supervision, and is considering policy updates to keep humans in control. Worth reading with the usual caution that an S-1 is a marketing document as well as a disclosure.

Source: TechCrunch, 2 November 2026, Anthropic warns that it's very close to RSI capability, aka self-building AI

The agentic web has arrived, and the open web is paying for it

Agents that browse, buy and transact on a person's behalf are now mainstream, with Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots and Anthropic's Claude Duet all reaching into email and bank accounts. The economics underneath are already shifting: Pew found users are about 40 per cent less likely to click through to a website when Google shows an AI summary, and Digiday puts the fall in publisher revenue from some ad formats at roughly a third between 2025 and 2026. Publishers are adapting rather than resisting, with Time now serving agent-friendly versions of articles and selling ads aimed at crawlers, while Amazon has simply blocked Muse and points shoppers at its own agent. The open question is whether this ends in a more useful web or a more concentrated one.

Source: MIT Technology Review, 2 November 2026, The agentic web is here

AI will shape the midterms more than the midterms talk about AI

The relaunched POLITICO tech show argues that AI is now a genuine force in the American political economy, already changing how people work, invest and travel, and yet it will not be near the top of most voters' minds at the ballot box. The hosts' conclusion is that the result will still carry real consequences for how AI gets built and deployed, which is why they frame the policy fight as outlasting any single election. The episode also points to new polling of national and Ohio voters on data centres, a local fight that has become the sharpest version of the national one.

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 3 November 2026, The forever AI wars

Models can now read and write neural network weights directly

A paper from Takehi and colleagues reports that frontier models can inspect and edit the weights of another neural network as if the weights were code. In the headline result, editing 0.016 per cent of the weights of Qwen3.5-0.8B lifted its MATH-500 accuracy from 7.9 per cent to 48.1 per cent. The framing that has people paying attention is that weight space programming would cut the slow autoresearch loop out of recursive self improvement entirely. It is early work and the result needs independent replication, but it lands in the same week Anthropic told its investors that accelerated progress may be imminent.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

A new agentic benchmark puts the best model at 12 per cent

SemiAnalysis released GDPx, a benchmark of 206 agentic tasks spread across 11 economic domains, and the top score was 12 per cent, from Opus 5.5. Gemini 4 Carbon came in at 10.9 per cent at roughly a quarter of the cost, which is the more interesting number for anyone budgeting an agent deployment. The gap between benchmark scores in the nineties on reasoning tests and single digit scores on real economic work is the point of the exercise. It is a useful corrective to the idea that frontier models are close to doing whole jobs.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

DeepSeek ships a subagent harness that nearly halves completion time

DeepSeek released dsh-superagent-prototype, an open harness that splits work across subagents rather than running one long session. Its own measurements put p95 completion time at 15.7 minutes against 29.5 minutes for a single session on the same tasks. The result matters less as a product than as evidence that orchestration, not model capability, is where a lot of near term agent performance now sits. Expect the pattern to be copied quickly.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

A one bit coding model keeps almost all of its accuracy

ISTA pruned DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash down to one bit per parameter across a 763 billion parameter mixture of experts model. The pruned version scores 88.57 on LiveCodeBench v6 against 89.71 for the base model, and 77.00 on SWE-bench Verified against 81.20. Commenters have disputed the evaluation setup, so treat the numbers as a claim rather than a settled result. If they hold, the cost of running a capable coding model on modest hardware falls sharply.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

Nvidia's Rubin generation claims a twentyfold gain in cost efficiency

SemiAnalysis reports that Rubin NVL72 delivers 20.9 times better performance per total cost of ownership than GB300 NVL72, measured at 325 tokens per second per user. For context on current pricing, 128 B300 capacity is going for about $5.75 per GPU hour, with the cards themselves around $300,000 each. A step change of that size, if it survives contact with real workloads, changes the arithmetic behind every inference budget written this year. It also explains why so much capital expenditure is being deferred rather than cancelled.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

Ninety performance patches in a day for about $230 in tokens

One developer pointed an agent at ASP.NET Core and produced roughly 90 performance pull requests in a single day, at a token cost of around $230. The figure is being passed around as a concrete price for autonomous contribution to a large open source codebase. The obvious caveat is that opening a pull request is not the same as having one merged, and maintainer review time is now the binding constraint. Still, it is the clearest cost benchmark yet for agentic code work at volume.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

Image and text models converge on the same representation without ever being paired

Work from Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun reports that separately trained image and text JEPAs arrive at matching internal representations without any paired training data. The practical claim is 42 times fewer samples needed for image and text alignment, 21.6 million against 900 million. If it generalises, it is evidence for the Platonic representation hypothesis, the idea that models of different modalities are converging on one underlying structure of the world. It would also make multimodal training substantially cheaper.

Source: Latent Space AI News, 3 November 2026, LLMs can read and write neural network weights

Does AI break the software moat, or widen it

Luca Ferrari, chief executive of Bending Spoons, makes the case that systematic incremental improvement compounds as reliably as breakthrough invention: acquire software companies, migrate them onto a shared platform, fix the unit economics, reinvest. Asked directly whether AI threatens software as a service, he answers through moats, brand, proprietary data, network effects and switching costs, and argues those hold. Internally the company uses AI across coding, support and data analysis rather than treating it as a product line. Worth noting that the host is an a16z general partner and the firm holds a large software book, so the view that software moats survive AI is not a disinterested one.

Source: the a16z Show, 1 November 2026, How Bending Spoons Built a $20 Billion Compounder, Luca Ferrari

AI slop moves the thinking onto whoever reads the work

Nufar Gaspar and Nathaniel Whittemore make an argument worth borrowing: AI slop is not just low quality output, it shifts the burden of thinking from the person producing the work to whoever receives it. Their practical answer is to form your own view before prompting, teach the model your taste rather than accepting its defaults, and build explicit review checkpoints into the workflow. Whittemore walks through how he applies that to producing a podcast with AI while keeping editorial control of its perspective. The habits are mundane, which is rather the point.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 2 November 2026, How to Deslopify Your AI
