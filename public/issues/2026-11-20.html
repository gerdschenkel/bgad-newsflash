20 November 2026

Commerce closes the cloud loophole with a 132 page export control rule

ChinaTalk ran an emergency episode on the rule Commerce released on 16 November, which for the first time goes after Chinese access to AI compute through cloud and remote hosting rather than only the physical export of chips. ChinaTalk puts roughly 40 percent of China's AI compute as arriving that way, so the rule targets the largest channel still open. Chris McGuire, former deputy senior director for technology at the National Security Council, noted that Commerce's own press release credited Treasury and the NSC with drafting it, which he and Julian Gewirtz read as a sign the department has little conviction about enforcing it.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 November 2026, Emergency Pod: BIS Finally Closes Loopholes!? What Will Xi Do Next?

The rule caps rented compute at about 200 Blackwell chips and reaches back in time

Chinese firms can now rent only around 200 top end Blackwell chips, or about 800 H100 equivalents, before a licence is required. Supplying 50 percent or more of your compute to Chinese customers becomes a violation, applied retroactively, which the guests say could expose Megaspeed. Two gaps stand out. The rule does not cover compute physically located in the United States, so a Chinese firm could simply shift to Northern Virginia, and a carve out for recommendation algorithms looks much like the one written for TikTok.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 November 2026, Emergency Pod: BIS Finally Closes Loopholes!? What Will Xi Do Next?

Chip licences extended to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil

Data centre operators in the five countries now need export licences for advanced AI chips, with existing facilities given 90 days to comply. Companies in those markets have displaced China as major Nvidia buyers, and Brazil is building capacity for ByteDance among others. McGuire expects the smuggling to migrate rather than stop, citing Johor's data centre capacity growing 56 fold between 2020 and 2024 and reports of hundreds of thousands of H200s sitting in the Port of Hong Kong. The AI Daily Brief added that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed the measure through over objections elsewhere in the administration.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 November 2026, Emergency Pod: BIS Finally Closes Loopholes!? What Will Xi Do Next?

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 17 November 2026, How Business AI Use is Changing

The foundry rule returns, and now covers any Chinese chip at 14 nanometres or better

Any Chinese company fabricating at 14 nanometres or more advanced needs a licence, whether or not the chip has anything to do with AI. McGuire's example is Xiaomi needing a licence for a 3 nanometre smartphone part. ChinaTalk's reading is that this closes the gap Huawei used to have roughly 3 million AI chips made at TSMC, which at about 15,000 dollars each comes to some 45 billion dollars of product.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 November 2026, Emergency Pod: BIS Finally Closes Loopholes!? What Will Xi Do Next?

The guests say penalties, not rule writing, are where export control has failed

McGuire and Gewirtz argue the text of the rules matters far less than whether anyone fears breaking them. They hold up the 500 million dollar fine Applied Materials paid for selling banned equipment to China as trivial next to its China revenue, and point to the 9 billion dollar sanctions penalty against BNP Paribas as the scale that actually changes corporate behaviour. Both want individual executives exposed, not only companies.

Source: ChinaTalk, 17 November 2026, Emergency Pod: BIS Finally Closes Loopholes!? What Will Xi Do Next?

Anthropic lists on Nasdaq in the largest raise the market has seen

Anthropic began trading on 19 November under the ticker ANT, priced at 83 dollars a share, above its marketed range, raising about 79 billion dollars at a valuation of 1.5 trillion. The stock rose more than 30 percent in the opening minutes and traded as high as 111.50 dollars, which put the company close to 2 trillion. The AI Daily Brief marked the day with a retrospective on the ten days that shaped generative AI, while Decoded by POLITICO looked at what a listed frontier lab does to the politics of regulating one, now that AI exposure sits inside ordinary retirement accounts.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 19 November 2026, The 10 Biggest Days in AI History

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 19 November 2026, Anthropic's world-changing IPO

Washington is set to name five companies as AI national champions

The AI Daily Brief relayed a Wall Street Journal report that the administration will designate Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic as national champions in AI. The label is said to carry expedited export licences and federal backing for international sales. Set against the same week's tightening of export controls, it points to an industrial policy that picks a short list of winners and then clears their path abroad.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 17 November 2026, How Business AI Use is Changing

Bob McGrew says superintelligence does not arrive without the physical world

OpenAI's former chief research officer told No Priors that automating AI research buys a one time step up rather than a runaway loop, because the binding constraints are physical: compute, GPUs, wet labs and factories. It is a pointed dissent from inside frontier research leadership, and it cuts against Paul Christiano's argument on the Dwarkesh Podcast three days earlier that such a loop could be close. McGrew now runs Arda, which is building a coding agent for manufacturing engineers and works with Anduril on components, missiles and the Fury aircraft.

Source: No Priors, 19 November 2026, Superintelligence Needs the Physical World with CEO and Co-Founder of Arda Bob McGrew

Nick Bostrom argues for pacing the frontier rather than pausing it

On a16z's podcast Bostrom made the case that the goal is keeping the option to slow down if safety work falls behind, not stopping, because a temporary pause can harden into lasting stagnation. He argues alignment gets harder as models become more situationally aware, and that some capability gains carry more risk than others, which points to selective restraint rather than a blanket halt. The venue is worth noting: a16z is a venture firm with a large AI portfolio and a public record of opposing restrictive AI rules, so the conclusion is a comfortable one for the house even though it is the guest's own.

Source: The a16z Podcast, 19 November 2026, How Fast Should We Build Superintelligence? Nick Bostrom

Support for banning recursive self improvement is spreading past the safety crowd

Decoded by POLITICO reported that backing for an outright ban on recursive self improvement, where a model upgrades its own capabilities with little human oversight, now reaches beyond the AI safety community into wider policy circles. The same episode covered Silicon Valley's push to return Jensen Huang to Nvidia running into a Washington that wants an investigation first. The part worth tracking is an RSI ban moving from a fringe position to a live legislative option.

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 17 November 2026, Silicon Valley's founder faith; is an RSI ban possible?

American and Chinese labs are quietly talking to each other about safety

A POLITICO exclusive reports that Concordia AI, a China based AI safety group, has been meeting top American AI companies including OpenAI, as well as United States policymakers. The group is described as central to a Chinese AI safety ecosystem that few outside it know anything about, and as one of the only working channels between researchers in the two countries. That it is running at all while the two governments fight over chips and national security is what makes it worth noting.

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 18 November 2026, Inside the AI labs' secretive U.S.-China safety meetings

Michael Tomasello says language models began at the finish line

The developmental psychologist, who spent 20 years running the same experiments on children and on great apes, told Machine Learning Street Talk that human intelligence rests on shared agency: joint goals, joint attention, and grasping that one object looks different from another vantage point. Models were trained on the finished output of that process without ever building the scaffolding underneath it, which he treats as a structural limit rather than something scale will fix. He also offers a design test for machine agency, arguing it only emerges where an environment is unpredictable enough that outcomes cannot be worked out in advance.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 19 November 2026, What It Would Actually Take for a Machine to Have Agency (Michael Tomasello)
