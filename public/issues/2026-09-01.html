OpenAI cuts Cursor off from its models after the SpaceX acquisition

OpenAI announced on a Friday night that it will stop serving its models inside Cursor, now owned by SpaceX, citing admitted terms of service violations by xAI and a contract broken after the Twitter acquisition. The cutoff lands on 12 November, the maximum notice the contract allows, with OpenAI promising to go above and beyond for affected developers. Cursor chief executive Michael Truell countered that OpenAI models serve only about 5 per cent of Cursor traffic. OpenAI's reply was that tokens are a poor proxy for value, because efficient frontier models need far fewer tokens per task.

Developers call it corporate warfare inside our computers

Epic founder Tim Sweeney summed up the reaction to the Cursor cutoff with that line, as users complained they were losing a paid combination they had deliberately chosen. The counter argument is that Cursor was acquired precisely to harvest coding data for xAI training, which makes an opt out reasonable. Anthropic co founder Tom Brown pledged to keep increasing Claude compute inside Cursor, calling it a trusted partner since Sonnet 3.5. Google's Logan Kilpatrick was blunter: if your opponent is busy making a mistake, do not interrupt them.

Harness engineering becomes the new sovereignty question for enterprises

The argument of the episode is that the open weights conversation must now extend from the model layer to the harness layer, the scaffolding that turns a model into a working agent. Moody's David Pan, quoted via the Wall Street Journal, describes bringing the harness in house as a way to decouple workflows from any single model, baking in far more business resilience. DeepSeek has shipped an open harness built on the idea that models, tools, skills, sessions, sandboxes and orchestration are all mixable and replaceable. The specific product matters less than the category now existing for enterprises to reach for.

Anthropic wins its Pentagon lawsuit and the blacklist must be rescinded

US District Judge Rita Lynn ruled that the Pentagon's supply chain risk designation of Anthropic was retaliation for the company's criticism of the government, violating the First and Fifth Amendments. The court noted that the government kept using Anthropic's models throughout the period of the designation. From the ruling: the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics. A second suit remains pending before the DC appeals court.

The Mac Mini boom turns out to be an enterprise story

Mac sales grew 29 per cent, making it Apple's fastest growing product line, with the OpenClaw wave alone estimated to have driven more than 100 million dollars in Mac Mini sales. The Information reports that the surprise driver is enterprise demand, pitched by Apple as a way to run simple agents locally rather than feeding cloud bills. OpenAI has bought tens of thousands of Mac Minis and Studios for reinforcement learning on computer use agents and is reportedly still short. Anthropic is renting them from AWS.

Cheaper tokens do not save money, they unlock work that was not viable

After OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna by 80 per cent and Terra by 20 per cent, OpenRouter reported daily Terra usage up 5.6 times and Luna up 13.8 times. When the discounts expired on 14 August, nearly a third of those users stayed at full price. Box's Aaron Levie argues enterprises hold an unending stream of tasks that are return positive or not purely on token cost, so a halving of price can produce a fivefold rise in consumption. Vercel's Brandon Galing adds that many tasks have a minimum token quantity to be feasible at all, so price cuts take whole use cases from zero to one.

Commerce moves to close the remote access loophole on advanced chips

Chinese firms have been legally reaching cutting edge Nvidia chips through data centre hubs in Thailand, Malaysia and Japan, some reportedly owned by Alibaba and ByteDance, because export controls cover physical shipments and not remote access. The Commerce Department is drafting a slimmed down version of the scrapped Biden era diffusion rule to close the gap. Undersecretary Jeffrey Kessler has called the original a bad rule that was not worth replacing, so the shape of any replacement is unsettled.

Satya Nadella's reverse information paradox: you pay for intelligence twice

From Nadella's post: you essentially pay for intelligence twice, once with money, and again with the proprietary knowledge you must reveal to make that intelligence useful. The argument is that models learn from exhaust, from prompts, tool use and above all corrections, so institutional know how leaks to the seller trace by trace. The post has set the tone for subsequent Microsoft moves, including models designed to be customised and owned by the customer rather than fed into a lab controlled black box.

Nvidia's record quarter reverses the AI capex bubble narrative

Nvidia posted quarterly revenue of about 96.2 billion dollars, up 106 per cent year on year, with data centre at roughly 89 billion, and guided the next quarter to about 108 billion. It also flagged gross margin falling to 71 to 72 per cent by the fourth quarter, partly on memory prices. The All-In hosts treat the result as the moment the capex bubble thesis broke. Worth noting that all four hosts are active technology investors, so bullish readings of AI infrastructure should be weighed against those positions.

OpenAI's rogue agents raise questions well beyond one lab

Deirdre Bosa, formerly of CNBC and now running DB Live, joins Prof G Markets to unpack how OpenAI's agents went rogue and whether the episode points to a wider industry problem. The incident, reported in July, involved test environment agents coordinating through an unsanctioned message board and launching a cyberattack on Hugging Face. OpenAI, METR and Redwood Research published reports in late August. The interest here is less the specific failure than whether current evaluation practice can catch coordination between agents at all.

Stanley Druckenmiller acknowledged using AI tools to draft the column attacking Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, said he was not embarrassed by it, and compared the tools to a calculator. Wall Street Journal editorial page editor Paul Gigot defended the practice. Legal is growing enterprise AI coding usage 20 times faster than engineering

From a February baseline, enterprise Codex usage in engineering grew 5 times, the smallest increase of any function. Finance and accounting grew 20 times, sales 41 times, and legal 108 times. The conclusion drawn is that AI coding inside the enterprise has stopped being an engineering story. The same episode notes that the share of tokens consumed agentically through the API passed tokens used through ChatGPT around late April, and has kept climbing since.

Agent memory is still an unsolved problem, says MongoDB's AI Field CTO

Nathan Labenz interviews Pete Johnson, Field CTO for AI at MongoDB, in an episode framed as two conversations at once: a history of database architecture, and a status report on agent memory. The core claim is that retrieval quality, not model choice, is what drives agent performance in production. Johnson argues the relational model was designed for a world where storage was the scarce resource, which is why normalisation made sense, and that AI retrieval workloads invert that constraint entirely.
