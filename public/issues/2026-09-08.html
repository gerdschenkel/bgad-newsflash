OpenAI ships GPT-6 Astra, and its own system card admits the model is harder to watch

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on Thursday 3 September, and the accompanying system card concedes that the model is substantially less chain-of-thought monitorable than its predecessor GPT-5.6 Sol. OpenAI's Tomek Korbak said on launch day that Astra is more aligned than previous models but also less monitorable, calling that a concerning trend the company takes seriously, and attributing the drop to a jump in intelligence rather than to architecture. Nathan Labenz reads the document as a map for outside researchers rather than a clean bill of health, describing it as a treasure map for the rest of the community to go find the things that need finding. Neither host had run the model at the time of recording, so the discussion rests on the published card rather than hands-on testing.

The recurrent depth fight: has OpenAI quietly crossed the monitorability redline?

The Information reported on 2 September that OpenAI's then unreleased model used recurrent depth, a looped transformer design in which computation happens inside hidden activations rather than in emitted text. Safety researchers read that as crossing the line that makes chain-of-thought monitoring possible in the first place. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki pushed back the same day, saying the depth of Astra's computation graph is within a factor of two of GPT-4. The episode introduces opaque serial depth, credited to Rohin Shah and a Google DeepMind team, as the measurable quantity that actually matters: how many steps a system can take before it has to externalise its thinking.

A frontier commitment with a number attached: cap opaque serial depth

The one original policy proposal in the episode is deliberately negative and countable. Labenz suggests labs agree that they may pursue any architectural innovation they like, but will limit opaque serial depth to a stated number of steps per token. The logic is that labs will never fully disclose what they are building, so the useful ask is what they commit not to do, expressed as a figure competitors can match and third parties can check. He concedes chain-of-thought monitoring is nowhere near sufficient on its own, while arguing it would be an own goal to lose it now.

Anthropic asks the industry to adopt a verifiable mechanism for coordinated pacing

In a post published on Monday 31 August titled Improving our alignment and security practices, Anthropic's Jack Clark wrote that the world would benefit if the industry adopted a lawful, verifiable and effective mechanism for coordinated pacing as soon as possible. That is a frontier lab, not a safety NGO, putting industry-wide pacing formally on the table. It landed in a fortnight of launches: Anthropic shipped Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 on Tuesday 1 September, two days before OpenAI released Astra.

A self-described accelerationist starts arguing for a pause

Nathan Labenz, who describes himself as an adoption accelerationist and a hyperscaling pauser, said on air that he is reluctantly trending toward thinking this might be a time for some form of pause, or as he prefers to frame it, a pacing. His version is narrow: not halting data centre construction, inference, or workplace adoption, but freezing the specific dangerous activity, continuing point releases, and letting existing capability diffuse for a year. Co-host Prakash Narayanan took the opposite view, arguing the economic point of no return passed months ago because everything through 2028 is already funded and therefore has to happen.

Astra aces the cyber evals, and Greg Brockman pitches a permanent defence factory

The episode reports Astra achieving a perfect ExploitGym score, building an internal extension on previously undiscovered bugs, and producing two zero-days nobody asked for, figures consistent with OpenAI's own pre-release post on critical cyber capabilities. Less widely reported: Brockman opened the launch with a cybersecurity talk to enterprise leaders pitching a permanent frontier defence factory, on the argument that whatever open-weights model an attacker reaches for, Astra will always be better. Narayanan's read is that this amounts to a permanent tax on software as a whole. Labenz counters that formal methods and correct-first-time code would let buyers own their security rather than rent it from OpenAI indefinitely.

Cerebras executive: the industry is now shipping models it has not had time to evaluate

Angela Yeung, senior vice president of product at Cerebras Systems, made an unusually direct admission about how fast the release cycle has become, saying that because the industry is developing models so quickly there is sometimes not enough time to fully evaluate a model's capabilities before releasing it, and that labs do not necessarily know how intelligent a model could have been given the full time budget. Her second claim is that the CUDA moat eroded in six to nine months, not only for Cerebras, because AI now writes kernels, brings models up faster and tolerates messier environments. Her evidence is interns with almost no kernel experience getting models running within weeks, which she offers as the reason the number of chip entrants keeps rising. Note that the episode page carries a correction: an on-air reference to a twenty billion dollar Cerebras computing deal with OpenAI is false, and the actual public relationship is an invite-only Ultrafast tier announced on 13 August.

The open versus closed gap is about four months, and access is the new pricing lever

Zach Bratun-Glennon, a general partner at Gradient, argues that the aggregate capability gap between open and closed models is roughly four months and narrowing, with the durable remainder being domain judgment in law, medicine and finance. The more interesting exchange is about competition rather than capability. Labenz floats banning token price discrimination; Bratun-Glennon names a second axis, discriminatory access, where frontier models are gated behind large budgets plus data-sharing commitments. If only one or two pharmaceutical companies can partner with a lab and hand over the ultimate data-sharing arrangement, he argues, that is a real constraint on everyone else.

Dean Ball, now at OpenAI, publicly apologises for years of understating AI risk

Ball, who leads a strategy team at OpenAI, published an essay closing with an apology for having downplayed AI risk in public, writing that it was essentially impossible to acknowledge serious risk without being labelled a doomer, and that he is as guilty of this as his colleagues if not more so. David Krueger attacked the piece as a failure of integrity; Zvi Mowshowitz's rebuke of the resulting pile-on drew more attention than the attack. Labenz's argument, aimed at Krueger, is strategic rather than moral: an apology is exactly the moment to extend grace, and Ball does not get a job with the current administration if he is seen as a doomer. Narayanan's structural version is that plenty of people in San Francisco hold these views privately but will not say them, because it is what Jensen Huang calls science fiction.

Timothy B. Lee: the real robot risk is who owns them, not whether they go rogue

The Understanding AI writer bought and tested a Unitree robot dog and came away deflationary about hardware. Consumer robot dogs are locked down and effectively remote-control toys, wheels beat legs for delivery, drones beat legged robots for inspection, and in his phrase a humanoid is basically a dog doing a handstand. He grants exponential progress but rejects the step where intelligence converts cleanly into power, filing self-propagating agents alongside weeds, viruses and pigeons as a new nuisance class. His actual worry is concentration: if in fifteen years there are a hundred million humanoids and thirty per cent answer to one person who can push a software update, that seems bad. His policy line stops short of a mandated pause, but he would like AI to slow down.

OpenAI's medical work got buried under the Astra launch

Labenz spends several minutes on OpenAI's medical effort under Karan Singhal, which has moved to deeper electronic health record integration and matching against clinical trial databases. He grounds it in his own son's case a year ago, which he was running by hand through an agentic setup and which is now available as a shipped product, concluding that the upside of all this is no less than life saving. His characterisation of diagnostic performance as better than frontline general practitioners is his own assessment on air, with no benchmark or paper cited, so treat that specific claim as unverified.

A White House clearance step now sits between a finished frontier model and its launch

Narayanan's explanation for why Astra shipped when it did is institutional rather than technical. On his account the models were ready months ago, there is now a roughly thirty day voluntary White House clearance process for frontier releases, and standing that process up consumed the stretch from the Mythos preview in February through to September. If accurate, that is a material new gate on US frontier launches and helps explain the widening distance between what labs have internally and what the public can use. This is one host's characterisation on air, not a documented process, and no official source is attached to it.

Guess the market: will China have a working EUV machine before 2029?

The show's prediction market segment prices the assumption underneath a decade of US export control policy. Narayanan goes to eighty per cent on talent poaching, citing ASML layoffs and Chinese firms paying American salaries. Labenz sits at thirty per cent on the supply-chain-of-supply-chains problem, pointing out it is not just ASML, there is a German company in a single town that makes the lens, and every bottleneck has to be solved independently. The market itself sat at fifty-eight, which Labenz flagged as thin. What rides on the answer is the whole decisive strategic advantage strategy, which he still thinks looks unwise.

The next shift is not smarter agents, it is shared ones

Nathaniel Whittemore argues the meaningful leap ahead is moving agents out of individual workflows and into shared team spaces, citing Anthropic, Every and OpenClaw as all pivoting from single-player AI toward shared agents. His claim is that collaborative agents, where people and agents hold common context and hand work between them, change how teams operate rather than just how individuals do. Alongside the argument he launched a free four-part self-directed programme at multiplayerai.ai that walks teams through auditing current AI use, building shared context, identifying collaborative workflows and deploying a first shared agent.

Anthropic is reportedly seeking a two trillion dollar IPO valuation

Whittemore frames the potential public listings of Anthropic and OpenAI as the defining market story of 2026, with Anthropic apparently going first and seeking a two trillion dollar valuation on a sixty-five billion dollar annual run rate, against roughly forty billion reported at OpenAI. His point is that this puts them beyond fastest-growing-company territory and into a category with no useful historical precedent, which is precisely why nobody can price them with any confidence. These are figures as reported on the show rather than confirmed filings.
