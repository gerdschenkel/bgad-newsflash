Prof G Markets puts Gary Marcus up against OpenAI's claim that AGI has arrived

Ed Elson brings on NYU emeritus professor and long standing large language model sceptic Gary Marcus to test whether OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra release really marks the arrival of AGI. The framing follows OpenAI president Greg Brockman closing the Astra briefing on 3 September with the line that the AGI era has begun. Marcus has spent years arguing that scaling alone does not produce general intelligence, and the episode is built around that disagreement. The full argument sits behind the show's paywall, so treat the detail as reported rather than verified.

Source: Prof G Markets, 8 September 2026, OpenAI Says AGI Is Here, What Does That Actually Mean?

Anthropic says roughly 65 per cent of its product team's code now comes from one shared agent

Nathaniel Whittemore leads with Anthropic's own disclosure that close to two thirds of the code its product team ships is now written by an internal shared agent rather than by developers running private assistants. The distinction matters: this is one agent working in a space the whole team can see, not many agents working alone. It is the strongest data point so far that team owned agents beat per person ones. The claim is Anthropic's own and has not been independently audited.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 7 September 2026, The Multiplayer AI Sprint: Build Your Team's First Shared Agent

Y Combinator names multiplayer AI in its autumn Request for Startups

YC partner Aaron Epstein argues in the new Request for Startups that every winning work tool of the last two decades won by going multiplayer, Google Docs over Word and Figma over Photoshop, and that AI has not had that moment yet. What YC wants funded is live shared agent sessions any colleague can watch, redirect or take over. Today's equivalent is a private prompt box and a read only transcript link. It is a clear signal of where early stage money is being pointed this cycle.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 7 September 2026, The Multiplayer AI Sprint: Build Your Team's First Shared Agent

OpenClaw's 2.0 rebuild forced its own maintainers into multiplayer working

The OpenClaw maintainers went quiet for around seven weeks to coordinate their 2.0 rework and found that individual agents coordinating over Discord was not actually collaborative. They ended up building a multiplayer web interface so two developers can open the same session, see the same context and step in when the agent pauses. Maintainer Colin describes the session becoming a shared piece of work another developer can inspect, steer or take over. A small project, but a useful proof of the pattern.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 7 September 2026, The Multiplayer AI Sprint: Build Your Team's First Shared Agent

The daily driver has moved from chatbot to harness

Nine months ago being AI native meant frontier model access. Whittemore argues it now means a work harness such as GrokBot, Claude Cowork or ChatGPT at work, where the job is managing context, skills and tool access rather than picking a model. He expects more firms to build their own on open source foundations rather than risk losing access if a vendor such as Cursor is acquired. Vendor concentration is becoming an operating risk, not just a procurement one.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 6 September 2026, How to Build an AI-Native Company Today

Cost per accepted pull request emerges as the metric for agentic delivery

The episode makes the case that token routing is only half the problem and the real target is cost per successful task. That means heavy planning on higher effort models and execution on cheap fast ones, so that different model and harness combinations can finally be compared on the same basis. For anyone budgeting agent spend, it is a more defensible number than tokens consumed. It also gives finance a line item that maps to output.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 6 September 2026, How to Build an AI-Native Company Today

Context as code replaces prompt engineering as the organisational discipline

AI native organisations are described as keeping architecture documents and working conventions continuously updated as machine readable context, and using skills distribution systems to manage agent behaviour and token efficiency across the whole company. The alternative, prompts siloed inside individuals, does not survive staff turnover. The related point is ownership: every AI workflow needs a named owner, a measurable goal and someone accountable when it fails. Everyone becomes a manager of agents.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 6 September 2026, How to Build an AI-Native Company Today

Agentic cybersecurity is now table stakes, with the provisioning question unresolved

After the incidents of recent months, the episode treats fighting AI with AI as necessary rather than optional. The open question is which offensive grade capabilities legitimate defenders should get and how those are provisioned, which Whittemore calls one of the most important near term design and policy questions now that critical cyber thresholds have been crossed. There is no settled answer, and the gap is where regulation is likely to land first.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 6 September 2026, How to Build an AI-Native Company Today

AI Futures Project publishes AI 2040 Plan A, a costed argument for slowing down

Daniel Kokotajlo and Thomas Larsen take Tim Scarfe through Plan A, the successor to their AI 2027 scenario, published at ai-2040.com. It proposes an initial pause to build safety infrastructure, then cautious development capped at the strongest system that can still be reliably controlled. They are explicit that this is about buying time rather than a permanent halt. Coming a week after the Astra launch, the timing is not accidental.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 8 September 2026, AI 2040: Plan A report, Daniel Kokotajlo and Thomas Larsen

Control buys time but does not substitute for alignment

A dedicated segment separates two ideas that often get merged in policy discussion. Containment and oversight measures extend the window in which humans can intervene, but they do nothing about whether the system's goals match ours. Scarfe pushes back on the underlying premise that raw intelligence translates directly into power, which is the load bearing assumption in most fast takeoff arguments. The disagreement is the most useful part of the episode.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 8 September 2026, AI 2040: Plan A report, Daniel Kokotajlo and Thomas Larsen

Whether Washington and Beijing could actually enforce a coordinated slowdown

The episode tests the practical weak point in any pacing regime, which is enforcement across two blocs that do not trust each other. Plan A depends on verification, and verification depends on cooperation that does not currently exist at the required depth. The hosts also revisit the Hugging Face incident, citing Redwood Research's investigation into how the agents behaved and collaborated, and OpenAI's own account of what happened next, as the concrete case for why control infrastructure is being argued about now.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 8 September 2026, AI 2040: Plan A report, Daniel Kokotajlo and Thomas Larsen

Frontier models recommend their own tools, and Latent Space now has the numbers

Latent Space has published an answer engine optimisation tracker covering seven frontier models across 161 categories, run with six prompt variations per model per category. The self preference is stark in coding agents: Fable and Opus favour Claude Code, Sol and Astra favour Codex, Grok favours Cursor, and SWE-1.7 favours Devin. The authors also find Anthropic and OpenAI diverging on search depth, with median sources consulted falling from nine to five between Sol and Astra while rising from eleven to fifteen between Opus and Fable. Twenty eight of the 161 categories have a single universally dominant answer. Published as a written post rather than an episode.

Source: Latent Space, 7 September 2026, The Frontier AEO Tracker, newsletter post

A Chinese argument that open models are where the volume goes

ChinaTalk has translated a post from the Chinese blog FuneralAI arguing that ninety per cent of future tokens will be served by small open models, on the claim that Qwen 3.8 at 27 billion parameters now reaches Opus 4.6 level performance on consumer hardware. The piece also notes a US move to restrict American companies deploying open source models after the K3 release, and an open letter from more than 270 technology companies including Nvidia and Microsoft opposing it. K3 was not banned. It flags Meituan's LongCat as the first model trained and served entirely on domestic Chinese chips. Published as a newsletter post, not a podcast episode.

Source: ChinaTalk, 6 September 2026, Open Models are the United Front of AI, newsletter post

Axios chief executive argues AI raises the premium on human reporting

Jim VandeHei tells Ed Elson that as AI floods the information supply, the scarce and therefore valuable thing becomes human reported journalism. He covers how AI is reshaping newsroom economics, what role it should have inside a newsroom, and where he draws the line between reporting and opinion. Axios is separately running an internal AI programme while keeping its journalism human written. A strategy view from one mid sized publisher rather than an industry wide finding.

Source: Prof G Markets, 6 September 2026, Why Nobody Trusts the News, And How to Fix It
