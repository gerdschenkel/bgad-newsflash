Trump says he is banning the words artificial intelligence from federal use

The President said this week that he is officially banning the terms AI and artificial intelligence from federal materials, calling it a very bad and inaccurate name, with agencies told to adopt a different label. Kara Swisher and Scott Galloway treat it as branding theatre, but note that executive orders, procurement rules and agency guidance are all written in exactly that vocabulary. The practical question is what happens to the policies and contracts that already carry the term.

Source: Pivot, 30 October 2026, Trump's War on Artificial Intelligence, Elon v. Scott, and Google's Wall Street Meltdown

The bubble argument is now being had in public, and the sceptics are split

Hard Fork gives its whole episode to the question, with New York Times reporters arguing that the Anthropic filing is the most detailed look anyone has had at whether AI revenue can ever catch the capital spending. On Prof G Markets, Josh Brown of Ritholtz Wealth Management takes the opposite side: if this is a bubble, he says, it is a feeble one, because investor behaviour does not resemble a classic mania. Brown adds that Anthropic's revenue growth genuinely impressed him, but that the company has little durable moat, since capability leads last months and switching costs are low.

Source: Hard Fork, 30 October 2026, Are We in an A.I. Bubble? We're About to Find Out

Source: Prof G Markets, 30 October 2026, Josh Brown: If This Is A Bubble, It's Pathetic

Steve Eisman's objection to the Anthropic listing is governance, not growth

The investor made famous by The Big Short told Prof G Markets he is not buying the Anthropic IPO, and his stated reason is the company's corporate governance structure rather than its numbers. That is a different line of attack from the usual bear case, which focuses on compute commitments and customer concentration. Ed Elson closes the same episode arguing the opposite, that the listing is widely misunderstood.

Source: Prof G Markets, 28 October 2026, Why Steve Eisman Isn't Buying Anthropic's IPO

Nvidia keeps buying stakes in the companies that buy its chips

Pivot returns to the circular financing question, with Nvidia reported to have taken a roughly three billion dollar position in Meta on top of its arrangements with OpenAI and SK. Jensen Huang has publicly rejected the circularity charge, arguing the investments are strategic rather than a way of funding its own demand. Swisher and Galloway treat the pattern as the clearest test of whether the AI capital cycle is standing on its own revenue.

Source: Pivot, 30 October 2026, Trump's War on Artificial Intelligence, Elon v. Scott, and Google's Wall Street Meltdown

A wave of PHP flaws took government sites offline, and attackers are already inside

Critical vulnerabilities in PHP, which still runs a large share of the web, pushed government websites in Europe, Canada and elsewhere offline from around 21 October. On 27 October the United States government confirmed that attackers are actively exploiting the flaws. Hard Fork covers it as a reminder that the least glamorous layer of the stack is still where the widest outages come from.

Source: Hard Fork, 30 October 2026, Are We in an A.I. Bubble? We're About to Find Out

WHOOP is refusing the FDA's demand to withdraw wrist blood pressure

Will Ahmed told All-In that the FDA sent WHOOP a warning letter telling it to stop offering wrist based blood pressure tracking, on the view that blood pressure readings require medical clearance. Ahmed has declined to pull the feature, arguing it is consumer wellness rather than a medical device. WHOOP's ECG feature does hold FDA clearance, which makes the dispute a line drawing exercise about where wellness ends and diagnosis begins. The outcome is not yet settled.

Source: All-In, 29 October 2026, WHOOP CEO, Will Ahmed: 50B Hours of Health Data, a 4 Million Member Data Moat, and the FDA Fight

WHOOP's case for selling a subscription instead of a 500 dollar device

Ahmed says the move from a roughly 500 dollar upfront device to a monthly membership was the most important decision in the company's history, because hardware businesses are forced to ship a new model every year whether or not it is better. A subscription let WHOOP add ECG, atrial fibrillation detection and blood pressure insights to devices customers already owned. He claims just over 50 billion hours of continuous physiological data across about four million members, who wear the device an average of 23.6 hours a day. All-In is hosted by working investors, and this is a founder interview rather than a news segment, so the claims are the company's own.

Source: All-In, 29 October 2026, WHOOP CEO, Will Ahmed: 50B Hours of Health Data, a 4 Million Member Data Moat, and the FDA Fight

AI safety groups are asking Newsom for a special legislative session

Advocates are pressing the California governor not to waste what they see as his last chance to tighten the state's AI rules before a presidential cycle in which he may be a candidate. California remains the effective national regulator of AI because that is where the major labs sit, so the timing question has consequences well beyond the state. The same episode previews the attorney general races that will decide how aggressively several states pursue the large platforms.

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 30 October 2026, AI safety advocates ask Newsom for special session; AG races to watch in the midterms

RAND gives Washington seven ways to argue about superintelligence

A new RAND framework sorts the strategy space into seven archetypes, including dominance, co-development, preparedness, moratorium, deterrence, continuity of society and acceleration, each with a defined win state and a characteristic failure mode. Dominance risks extreme concentration of power and value lock in; enforcing a moratorium risks hardening into global surveillance. The authors also set out five pivot factors designed to expose the assumptions behind any position, such as whether danger is close and whether restraint is feasible. They have begun costing a catalogue of 106 security capabilities, on the argument that a strategy without a price tag is just a vision.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 28 October 2026, Freedom of Action: America's ASI Strategy, with RAND's Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux and Matt Chessen

The near term risk may be swarms of weaker agents rather than one strong model

RAND's Matt Chessen argues the field is watching the wrong shape of threat, and that superintelligent behaviour is more likely to emerge from large numbers of cooperating sub-AGI agents than from a single system. He points to this year's agent driven intrusions as early evidence, and forecasts a rough patch in cyber over the next twelve to twenty four months as strong offensive capability reaches open weight models. The policy response he favours is not doctrine but capability: verification, containment of untrusted models, and evaluations that catch a model behaving differently when it knows it is being tested.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 28 October 2026, Freedom of Action: America's ASI Strategy, with RAND's Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux and Matt Chessen

A legal AI company claims it takes a lawyer from 100 clients to 500

Eve co-founder Jay Madheswaran told the a16z podcast that some plaintiff firms using its software have lifted the number of clients each attorney can handle from roughly 100 to about 500, by automating the workflow from first call through settlement. His wider argument is that AI makes a class of cases economically viable that firms previously could not afford to touch, so the effect is more access to representation rather than fewer lawyers. Worth weighing carefully: a16z led Eve's Series A, the interviewer is a partner at the lead investor, and the productivity figure is the founder's own.

Source: the a16z Podcast, 30 October 2026, Why AI Could Turn Small Law Firms Into Billion-Dollar Businesses

Serval is pointing coding agents straight at ServiceNow's moat

Jake Stauch told No Priors that his company lets enterprise IT teams, including people who do not write code, build their own automations rather than paying for a workflow layer and the consultants who configure it. The control story is deterministic approvals and audit trails, so agent written automation still clears compliance. His explicit bet is that coding agents keep improving, so the product should be built for the agent it will have rather than the one it has, which is a sharper statement of the thesis than most founders will make on the record.

Source: No Priors, 29 October 2026, Taking on ServiceNow: Coding Agents for the Rest of Us, with Serval co-founder Jake Stauch

Supabase is rebuilding its developer setup for agents rather than people

At its Select event the company announced an alpha runtime that runs the full backend locally without Docker, and declarative schemas that keep the desired database state in files that can be committed to Git. Both are aimed at coding agents, which need a cheap local environment and a diffable source of truth rather than imperative migrations. A private alpha called Supabase Compute runs work such as chunking and embedding next to the database instead of in a separate service.

Source: Latent Space, 28 October 2026, Why Agents Love Supabase

Five million databases a month, and models as the distribution channel

The founders say Supabase is now launching roughly five million databases a month, which is the cleanest public number anyone has put on the volume of agent generated applications. Swyx reports that his answer engine tracker found Supabase was the first recommendation for app backends across essentially every frontier model he tested. The founders add that activation and conversion improved as AI driven signups grew, because an agent can carry a user from signup to a working setup in one pass. These are the company's own figures.

Source: Latent Space, 28 October 2026, Why Agents Love Supabase
