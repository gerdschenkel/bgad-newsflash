Bankers are pressing rating agencies to hand OpenAI and Anthropic investment grade on day one

Ed Elson opened with same day Financial Times reporting that Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs are lobbying Fitch, Moody's and S&P to grant investment grade ratings effective immediately after the two labs list. The prize is access to the roughly 11.7 trillion dollar corporate bond market, where pension funds and insurers can only buy rated paper. Neither company has stable positive free cash flow. Elson called the approach a red flag; FT columnist Katie Martin replied that pre rating conversations are ordinary practice, while conceding it "can look a little bit iffy from the outside."

Source: Prof G Markets, 11 September 2026, Why The Bond Market Is Starting To Revolt, ft. Katie Martin

Hyperscaler borrowing is now crowding governments out of the bond market

Martin traced a shift that took about 18 months. The large hyperscalers used to fund AI capital expenditure from free cash flow, ran out, and moved to debt. That new corporate supply is pulling demand away from government bond markets at exactly the moment governments face record issuance, because an investor choosing between a Treasury and a Meta bond of similar duration can simply take the more generous corporate paper. Smaller European governments have already rescheduled planned issues to avoid landing on the same day as a hyperscaler deal. Martin called it one of the most underappreciated stories in global finance right now.

Source: Prof G Markets, 11 September 2026, Why The Bond Market Is Starting To Revolt, ft. Katie Martin

If you hold an index fund, you already run an active tech position

Martin's argument is that AI concentration has spread well past US equities into private markets, private credit and public corporate bonds, and is embedded in index exposure worldwide. Her example: AI is effectively the entire Korean stock market, plus large parts of the Taiwanese and Japanese markets, so anyone with a pension or a country index ETF is holding a tech fund whether they chose one or not. Elson added the loop that worries him, which is US growth depending on AI, AI depending on a handful of firms, and those firms' spending being funded in large part by OpenAI and Anthropic revenue. Martin declined to call a crash: "the music's still playing, so people are still dancing."

Source: Prof G Markets, 11 September 2026, Why The Bond Market Is Starting To Revolt, ft. Katie Martin

Galloway on the Hugging Face breach: hold the chief executives liable, not the model

Asked which story had been most badly framed this summer, Scott Galloway named the Hugging Face incident. He argued the idea that these systems are sentient actors conspiring on their own is, in his words, a narrative designed to absolve their owners of responsibility, and one that conveniently doubles as a pitch for how powerful the product is. His analogy came from running a university interfraternity council, where noise violations only stopped once police began arresting the fraternity president overnight. Applied to AI: hold Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Satya Nadella accountable for what their bots do and they will find a way to control them.

Source: Pivot, 11 September 2026, Trump's 9/11 Fiction, a Major AI Warning, and Apple's Foldable iPhone

Kara Swisher has taken Google off her phone and published the instructions

Swisher said she has removed Google Maps and Google Search from her devices in protest at how Google and Apple accommodated the Trump administration over the Gulf renaming, and has published step by step instructions so listeners can do the same. She has moved to DuckDuckGo for search, MapQuest for maps, which she admits is missing features such as subways, and the Vivaldi browser in place of Safari. Her claim on the AI substitution is worth noting for anyone tracking search displacement: "using DuckDuckGo and Claude together works beautifully. It replaces Google search completely." She conceded the limit of the protest, since on hardware she remains locked into Apple.

Source: Pivot, 11 September 2026, Trump's 9/11 Fiction, a Major AI Warning, and Apple's Foldable iPhone

The second mouse gets the cheese: Galloway bets Apple outsells seven years of foldables in 60 days

Galloway conceded Apple's foldable is derivative rather than inventive, then predicted that within 60 days of shipping Apple will sell more units than every foldable phone sold in the previous seven years, leaving Samsung furious at having gone first. The numbers from the episode: 2,000 dollars entry price before storage upgrades, 5.4 inches closed and 7.6 inches open, and no units until October. Swisher's counterweight is that foldables remain under 2 per cent of global smartphone shipments and that screen durability is still the unresolved risk.

Source: Pivot, 11 September 2026, Trump's 9/11 Fiction, a Major AI Warning, and Apple's Foldable iPhone

Data centre opposition is producing coalitions that cut across party lines

With the Hard Fork team away, the feed carried an Ezra Klein Show interview with journalist Jasmine Sun about a reporting trip through the American Midwest looking at how local resistance to data centre construction actually works on the ground. The finding that matters for anyone planning infrastructure: the opposition is not partisan, and residents do not believe the economic promises being made to them. Sun's underlying essay is "No Data Centers In My Backyard." Note that this is a feed drop rather than new Hard Fork content, and the conversation itself was recorded in early August.

Source: Hard Fork, 11 September 2026, The Ezra Klein Show: The A.I. Revolt Is Here

OpenAI ships GPT-Live 1 and pushes real time voice and vision into live work

The lead item on Friday's AI Daily Brief is OpenAI's GPT-Live 1, which Nathaniel Whittemore frames as widening the set of jobs that can be handed to a live multimodal system rather than a turn taking chatbot. The applications he names are customer service and sales, education, healthcare and hands free work. Detail is thin at this stage, with no pricing or availability in the show notes, so treat capability claims as provisional until OpenAI publishes specifics.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 11 September 2026, What to Use the Latest AI Tools For

Cognition ships SWE-2 and Cursor adds Projects in the same week

Two coding tool releases landed alongside the model news. Cognition, valued at 48 billion dollars earlier this week, has shipped SWE-2, and Cursor has added a Projects feature. Neither is accompanied by benchmark numbers or feature detail in the episode, so this is a signal about release cadence in the agentic coding market rather than a capability comparison. The pattern worth watching is that the tooling layer is now shipping as fast as the model layer.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 11 September 2026, What to Use the Latest AI Tools For

Brian Armstrong wants crypto rails to be the settlement layer for AI agents

Armstrong joined Elad Gil to argue that autonomous agents and crypto infrastructure are converging, because agents transacting on their own behalf need programmable, always available settlement that card networks and bank hours do not provide. He sets that against a broader plan to make Coinbase an "everything exchange" spanning tokenised real world stocks, stablecoin payments and prediction markets. He also discussed how internal AI use is changing the relationship between output and headcount at a large regulated fintech, though no figures were given. Read the agentic commerce framing as a strategic bid rather than a settled market structure.

Source: No Priors, 10 September 2026, Coinbase's Everything Exchange: Agentic Finance, Stablecoins, and Tokenization with CEO Brian Armstrong

Nathan Labenz proposes an actual goal for US AI policy, and calls it Pax Machina

In a three hour solo finale to his China series, Labenz argues that American AI discourse almost never states what it is trying to achieve, and offers one: a Pax Machina, or as he also puts it, a Pax Robotica. The episode is timed to Xi Jinping's 24 September visit to Washington and the first dedicated bilateral AI talks led by Bessent. He recorded his China meetings under Chatham House rules, so no counterparts are named. The show notes are AI generated from the transcript, which is worth knowing when weighing the detail.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 10 September 2026, Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal and the Road to Pax Robotica

A black market for Claude tokens has appeared in China

Labenz reports that restricted API access has produced a resale market for Claude tokens inside China, alongside continued distillation of Western models. This is a demand side consequence of export and access controls rather than a capability story, and it suggests the controls are binding in practice rather than merely on paper. Treat this as his on air assertion: no volumes, pricing or mechanism are given in the show notes.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 10 September 2026, Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal and the Road to Pax Robotica

Export controls have quietly frozen US and Chinese safety researchers out of talking to each other

An unintended effect Labenz reports first hand from his trip: American AI safety organisations are declining to work with Chinese counterparts for fear of export control charges. His view is that these organisations are treating cautious legal advice as though it were binding law, and that the chilling effect is self imposed rather than required. It is a distinct problem from the enforcement question, because it removes the very channel through which any coordinated arrangement would have to be negotiated.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 10 September 2026, Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal and the Road to Pax Robotica

Kimi K3 and Meituan's LongCat are the two data points that settle the chip controls argument

Labenz's audit of chip export controls rests on two cited cases pulling in opposite directions. Kimi K3 suspending new paid signups is, in his framing, the constraint that did bind, evidence that compute scarcity is real for Chinese labs. Meituan's LongCat, trained on domestic silicon, is the existence proof that the constraint can be routed around given time. Both are cited reporting in the show notes rather than original findings, so verify before quoting either as fact.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 10 September 2026, Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal and the Road to Pax Robotica

What a US and China AI deal would actually contain: joint compute, auditor rights, weapons limits

Labenz closes with three concrete mechanisms rather than a call for cooperation in the abstract. Keep the two countries' technology trees entangled and pace the frontier jointly; establish an auditor's bill of rights, extending Anthropic's Clio confidential computing pattern so third party auditors can inspect systems without seeing user data; and negotiate a convention on lethal autonomous weapons building on the existing Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons talks. He also proposes pricing the risk of deploying Chinese open weight models through an insurance market rather than banning them.

Source: The Cognitive Revolution, 10 September 2026, Nathan Goes to China #3: US-China Relations, the Art of the AI Deal and the Road to Pax Robotica
