The midterm results turn the politics of AI upside down

Two shows read Tuesday's results the same way, as a defeat for the technology industry's political position rather than for one party alone. POLITICO's Steven Overly argues that Democrats who campaigned against Big Tech won decisively, and that the long alignment between Republican candidates and the industry may have reached its end point. Nathaniel Whittemore's reading is that the new scrutiny lands hardest on data centre construction and on AI regulation, and that the likely result is local communities extracting a better deal rather than the build out stopping. Neither episode puts numbers on the shift, so treat the scale of it as an early read.

Source: Decoded by POLITICO, 4 November 2026, Big Tech goes down with GOP

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 4 November 2026, How the Midterm Results Change AI

Robots are already on American factory floors at about $16 an hour

Tutor Intelligence chief executive Josh Gruenstein describes putting robots into working factories at roughly $16 an hour, with remote human operators taking over whenever a robot gets stuck. Every one of those interventions becomes training data, which the company runs through a 100 robot operation it calls a data factory. Gruenstein's argument is that robotics is a systems problem spanning hardware, manufacturing and the shape of the work itself, not a model problem waiting on a better model. He is a founder speaking on a venture firm's show alongside a16z partners Martin Casado and Raghu Raghuram, so the hourly figure is a vendor claim rather than an audited one.

Source: the a16z Show, 2 November 2026, The Robots Already Working in American Factories, Tutor Intelligence

A legal AI chief says today's models already handle about 60 per cent of legal work

Jacob Lauritzen, chief technology officer of Legora, estimates that frontier models already do roughly 60 per cent of legal work to a standard lawyers will accept. His more pointed claim is about the remainder: he does not think better scaffolding, prompting or retrieval closes the gap, and says the rest needs a genuinely smarter model. That is an unusual position from a company whose product is largely scaffolding. The number is his own and unaudited, and he is describing his own product.

Source: Latent Space, 2 November 2026, Building Legal Agents When You Cannot See the Data, Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora

Citation accuracy jumped from about 70 per cent to about 99 per cent in one release

Legora's citation feature was getting references right about 70 per cent of the time, which in legal work is close to useless. After a single model release it was correct about 99 per cent of the time, with nothing else in the system changed. Lauritzen pairs that with a blunter observation from the same episode, which is that lawyers do not reliably check citations anyway. It is a clean illustration of how much of an applied AI company's roadmap is really a bet on the next model landing.

Source: Latent Space, 2 November 2026, Building Legal Agents When You Cannot See the Data, Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora

How do you debug an agent you are not allowed to watch

Legora cannot read the traces of its own product, because what its agent handles belongs to law firms' clients. So the team tests against simulated environments modelled on real customer work, including a synthetic data room of 10,000 files, and inspects failures only in paraphrased and grouped form rather than reading them. That has narrowed observed failures to somewhere between 10 and 20 recurring types. For anyone deploying agents on confidential material, this is the most practical engineering pattern in the week's listening.

Source: Latent Space, 2 November 2026, Building Legal Agents When You Cannot See the Data, Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora

A crowd sourced meme benchmark ranks MiniMax above ByteDance

Xianyu, who writes the satirical Chinese AI newsletter Funeral AI, built a site that ranks Chinese video generation models by how meme worthy their output is, paying around 300 university students roughly 10 yuan a task to do the ranking. MiniMax's H3 came out above ByteDance's Seedance 2.0 and 2.5, which runs against the industry's prevailing view, though he separately puts H3 at about 80 per cent of Seedance 2.0 on conventional metrics. The underlying point is that the benchmarks a market agrees on decide which labs get capital, and China's are still largely imported. This episode is the Chinese language edition of an interview ChinaTalk first ran in English on 29 October.

Source: ChinaTalk, 3 November 2026, Roasting China's AI Industry

A frontier lead lasts a month or two, because model building is really manufacturing

Xianyu's argument is that large model development rests on compute and data with very few fundamental secrets, which is why no lab holds a lead for more than a month or two before the others catch up. He reads it as a manufacturing business rather than a research one. On demand he cites ByteDance's Doubao reaching 200 to 300 million users in China and says compute demand keeps climbing, while allowing for a short term slowdown. He also describes Chinese startup funding rotating themes roughly every three months, following whichever category Silicon Valley has just named.

Source: ChinaTalk, 3 November 2026, Roasting China's AI Industry

The Nasdaq slid into correction, and the call is already that it is over

Two consecutive Prof G Markets episodes bracket the AI trade's worst stretch in months. On Tuesday, Deirdre Bosa walked through why the Nasdaq fell into correction territory and what the mood now feels like in San Francisco. On Wednesday, Fundstrat's Tom Lee argued the sell off has run its course and that stocks are about to surge, with a year end forecast the show notes reference but do not state. Lee is a persistently bullish strategist and this is a market call, not a result, so it is worth weighing against the capex anxiety that caused the drawdown.

Apple's results land, and sitting out the AI arms race keeps looking smart

Patrick McGee, author of Apple in China, returns to Prof G Markets to work through Apple's latest earnings and how the new iPhone Duo is actually selling. It lands in the same week Scott Galloway repeated that he continues to be impressed by Apple's refusal to join the AI capital expenditure race, a position that looked complacent a year ago and now looks like discipline. The contrast with the hyperscalers is the story: Apple is being rewarded for the spending it did not do. The episodes do not publish McGee's verdict or any sales figures in their notes.

Grok Bot's costs reopen the question of who is paying for AI

Nathaniel Whittemore picks up Grok Bot's rising costs as the week's clearest evidence that AI products are being sold well below what they cost to serve. Elon Musk's own explanation for users suddenly hitting weekly limits, on free and paid plans alike, was that the cost of serving Grok Bot was unsustainably high relative to revenue. The interesting part is not the limits but the admission, which puts a number of consumer AI subscriptions in a different light. No pricing detail or cost figure has been published alongside it.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 3 November 2026, AI Lurches Toward Recursive Self-Improvement

Source: Technowize, 3 November 2026, Elon Musk Cites Unsustainably High Costs For Stricter Grok Bot Usage Limits

About $6 billion gone from Binance, and the blockchain was not the weak point

Kara Swisher and Scott Galloway took the Binance theft to the Beacon Theatre stage, covering what is being reported as the largest crypto heist on record at roughly $6.3 billion. North Korea has been blamed in reporting, and the breach came through multiple points rather than any failure of the chain itself. Popular Mechanics makes the same point more bluntly, that the cryptography held and the ordinary operational layer around it did not. It is a reminder that custody, key management and staff access remain the actual attack surface.

Google gets a working prototype for data centres in orbit

Google's Project Suncatcher has flown a prototype satellite that successfully beamed data back to Earth, in this case a short haiku about being in space. The plan behind it is to put machine learning compute into orbit, where solar power is continuous and cooling is free, which addresses two of the three constraints now slowing terrestrial data centre build out. The third constraint, cost of launch and servicing, is the one the prototype does not answer. Worth watching alongside the local political resistance to data centres that the midterm coverage keeps surfacing.

Source: PCMag, 31 October 2026, Google Scores Big Win in Quest to Put Data Centers in Space

Being flagged as AI written is becoming a real professional risk

NPR follows a ghostwriter who lost work after a detector flagged her proposal as AI generated, and uses it to examine what these tools actually measure. Detectors such as Pangram score statistical regularity, which is not the same thing as authorship, and human writing that is clean and conventional scores much like model output. For organisations now running detectors over job applications, student work and supplier deliverables, the operational question is what happens to the person on the wrong end of a false positive. There is currently no agreed standard of evidence.

Source: NPR, 31 October 2026, Your writing was flagged as AI. What does that actually mean?

Is consciousness just a learning algorithm about as complex as a transformer

Joscha Bach puts forward what he calls his null hypothesis, that consciousness is a learning procedure simple enough for an infant brain to discover unaided, and not much more complicated than a transformer. He splits it into two claims that can fail separately: that consciousness is a biological learning algorithm, and that machines learning in self organising ways might find the same one. He also argues there may never be a Turing test for it, because faking a mind can be harder than building one. The question of what would actually count as evidence is live elsewhere too, with philosophers pushing back on exactly this framing.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 2 November 2026, Why Leibniz Could Not Find the Mind Inside the Mill, Joscha Bach

Source: Philosophy News, 31 October 2026, What Counts as Evidence for AI Consciousness?
