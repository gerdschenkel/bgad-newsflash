Musk confirms Tesla and SpaceX boards are studying a merger

Elon Musk has said special committees at both Tesla and SpaceX are evaluating a combination, telling investors there is "a lot of merit to combining the companies". Scott Galloway and Ed Elson expect the boards to approve it and argue the structure is what makes it attractive to Musk: because SpaceX carries dual class shares, his voting power in the combined company would go from roughly 20 percent of Tesla's votes to roughly 80 percent. Galloway called it a real merger with a fake promise. The hosts also noted prediction market Kalshi pricing an 81 percent chance of a deal before 2028, and flagged that a stock for stock structure could push the S&P index committee into an eligibility exception, routing passive money into a company that never held a conventional listing.

Source: Prof G Markets, 26 October 2026, The $3.5T Bailout: Elon's Plan To Merge Tesla And SpaceX

Data centre financing is visibly seizing up

Elson reported that since September eight data centre listings have been pulled, postponed or repriced, with Firmus cancelling its IPO, SP Energy delaying its own, and one data centre company falling more than 30 percent on its first day of trading. Credit default swap spreads on AI linked names including Oracle have widened to record levels. Neocloud equities moved with it, with Nebius and CoreWeave both down sharply over the month. The pattern points at the financing layer rather than demand as the place where stress is showing up first.

Source: Prof G Markets, 26 October 2026, The $3.5T Bailout: Elon's Plan To Merge Tesla And SpaceX

Meta's own filing warns capital is getting harder to raise

The hosts quoted Meta's quarterly filing describing "an increasingly challenging environment for raising capital", while putting combined hyperscaler capital spending at roughly 750 billion dollars this year across Meta, Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet. Meta alone is at about 145 billion this year with a possible 800 billion over several years. Galloway still named Meta as the company he expects to win the AI build out over three years, which is the tension worth watching: the heaviest spender is also the one telling regulators the money is getting more expensive.

Source: Prof G Markets, 26 October 2026, The $3.5T Bailout: Elon's Plan To Merge Tesla And SpaceX

Idle GPUs and rising cloud margins tell different stories

Citing Business Insider, the hosts said Amazon is spending billions of dollars a month on Nvidia racks it cannot install fast enough, leaving hardware sitting in warehouses while its depreciation clock runs. The constraint has moved from chip supply to power, shells and skilled labour. Elson put the counter case: AWS operating margin has gone from 33 percent to 39 percent and Google Cloud from 21 percent to 36 percent, and margin expansion of that size usually signals constrained supply rather than a glut. Ramp card data showing corporate spend on AI tokens slowing sits on the other side of that argument.

Source: Prof G Markets, 26 October 2026, The $3.5T Bailout: Elon's Plan To Merge Tesla And SpaceX

Most of Anthropic's reported loss turns out to be an accounting entry

Elson said the reported Anthropic figures show roughly 16 billion dollars of revenue against an 80 billion dollar loss, but about 72 billion of that is a non cash item tied to convertible debt, putting the underlying operating loss nearer 9 billion. That is a materially different company from the one the headline number describes, and it matters ahead of a listing that several shows now treat as the swing event for the AI trade. Galloway's prediction for the filing was 60 pages on the business and 100 pages on the risks.

Source: Prof G Markets, 26 October 2026, The $3.5T Bailout: Elon's Plan To Merge Tesla And SpaceX

OpenAI says its agents now use software better than the average person

Ari Weinstein and James Sun, who lead OpenAI's computer use work, say agents have crossed a reliability threshold and now operate software more reliably and more quickly than an average human user, with superhuman performance plausible in the near term. They credit combining signals rather than relying on one: screenshots, accessibility trees, direct access to the page structure, and generated code. The commercial argument is reach rather than novelty, with the Agents API pitched as the way into legacy enterprise systems that were never built to be integrated, starting in finance and procurement. Worth noting that this runs on Andreessen Horowitz's own feed, the hosts are a16z partners, and the firm has published research arguing these unit economics already work.

Source: The a16z Podcast, 26 October 2026, OpenAI: When AI Agents Use Computers Faster Than You

OpenAI's agent leads say the user interface is not going away

Against the view that agents will swallow the application layer, Weinstein and Sun argue people will keep wanting to touch software directly, and that interface design will keep changing as human preference and agent capability develop together. They also say how much of the result comes from the surrounding harness rather than the model itself, which is an unusual admission from the team selling the model. It is a direct counterweight to the argument Ben Horowitz made on the same feed two days earlier, that the application layer matters less once agents work the data directly.

Source: The a16z Podcast, 26 October 2026, OpenAI: When AI Agents Use Computers Faster Than You

Box's Aaron Levie puts a new agent at about 1,000 dollars against 100,000 to hire

Levie's claim is that a team of three to ten people can now produce what used to need fifty to two hundred, and that the cost comparison is the reason: hiring a person runs 100,000 to 300,000 dollars, while standing up an agent might cost around 1,000. The conclusion he draws is not headcount reduction but new demand, because companies will hand agents work they would never have hired anyone to do. He contrasts it with founding Box in 2005, when progress was entirely a function of hours spent typing, and says editing AI written code carries roughly ten times the leverage of writing it. The investor caveat applies: this is a16z's feed and a16z's thesis.

Source: The a16z Podcast, 25 October 2026, The New Startup Playbook, Box CEO Aaron Levie

Agent pricing unlocks work nobody could afford, and Levie still calls agents a slot machine

His worked example is legal review, where work priced at 1,000 to 2,000 dollars an hour today could run at 5 to 10 dollars an hour with an agent, releasing volumes the price was suppressing. He is blunt about the state of the technology, describing agents as a slot machine, error prone and not a cure for anything, and says incumbents struggle to capture the gains because their workflows are welded to the old way of working. He prefers specialised sub agents to one general one, and argues distribution still decides the winner: a great agent on its own gets drowned out by ten thousand others.

Source: The a16z Podcast, 25 October 2026, The New Startup Playbook, Box CEO Aaron Levie

Machine Learning Street Talk names the weak point in machine generated proofs

The show's argument is that when an AI system produces a proof no human can read, the real question is not whether the checker passed but whether the formal statement it certified is the theorem anyone meant. It calls that distance the formalisation gap. Lean creator Leonardo de Moura, Jeremy Avigad, Kevin Buzzard and Paul Lessard all appear, with Lessard separating a proof that is correct from one that is useful. This matters commercially because AI labs increasingly cite Lean verified output as evidence of reasoning ability, and the episode says the verification does not reach as far as the claim implies.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 26 October 2026, Mathematics Just Changed Forever, Kevin Hartnett

Formalising mathematics still costs about twenty times the informal proof

The episode puts the de Bruijn factor, the ratio of machine checkable code to the informal mathematics it encodes, at roughly 20 to 1 or higher, and treats that overhead as the practical reason the field has not been formalised despite Lean and Mathlib existing. Thomas Hales spent around twenty years formalising his Kepler conjecture proof; Peter Scholze asked Kevin Buzzard's team to check a six page proof from one of his own papers and it took six months. That ratio is the number any claim about AI automating mathematics has to beat. The same machinery is now being pointed at shipped software, including a verified zlib implementation, which is where the gap stops being academic.

Source: Machine Learning Street Talk, 26 October 2026, Mathematics Just Changed Forever, Kevin Hartnett

Nathaniel Whittemore's weekly round up puts four questions at the centre of the week: whether China has closed the frontier gap after Moonshot's Kimi K3.1, whether AI driven cyber chaos is now the permanent state of the internet rather than a spike, whether data centre funding is drying up, and what happens to the whole trade if Anthropic's listing disappoints. Elon Musk's warning on debt markets is the hook for the third. Taken together it is the same financing anxiety showing up across the investor shows this week, arriving from the model and security side instead.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 26 October 2026, 4 Questions for AI This Week

Data centres are becoming an issue in US election campaigns

Pollster Kristen Soltis Anderson told Kara Swisher that candidates are starting to talk about data centres in their advertising, while cautioning that she has never seen a poll where they register as a voter's first concern. David Wasserman of the Cook Political Report said candidates in Iowa and Georgia have adopted opposition to data centres, and framed it as a possible test for Democrats on fighting large AI companies. This is the channel through which the build out actually gets slowed: local siting, power and water fights moving up into national politics. Anderson expects AI itself to be a first order issue by the 2028 campaign.

Source: Pivot, 25 October 2026, Pivot Extra: From 'Trump Curious' to 'Trump Furious': Inside the Midterm Odds

Pollsters say prediction markets are still effectively unpoliced

Terrance Woodbury of HIT Strategies described prediction markets as a largely unregulated industry where people with inside information are still trading, and Wasserman said there is nothing anyone can realistically do to police it at this point. Wasserman added a commercial side effect: the Cook Political Report is now getting subscription interest from people actively wagering on races. The context is that Kalshi and Polymarket prices are quoted as news across the financial and tech press, including in this week's Tesla and SpaceX merger coverage.

Source: Pivot, 25 October 2026, Pivot Extra: From 'Trump Curious' to 'Trump Furious': Inside the Midterm Odds

Buying drones achieves nothing without units trained to fight with them

Michael Kofman's argument on ChinaTalk is that the defence procurement conversation has the problem backwards. Acquiring drones delivers little unless militaries also build drone platoons and companies trained to fight with them, which is an organisational and doctrinal problem rather than a hardware one. He pairs it with the observation that cheap thermal and night capable sensing has erased the night vision advantage Western doctrine was built around, and that the US military has no real culture of decoys. For anyone tracking defence technology spending, the bottleneck has moved from manufacturing to force structure.

Source: ChinaTalk, 25 October 2026, WarTalk: Kofman on Starlink and What Ukraine Can Teach the U.S. Military
