Dario Amodei has put a concrete slowdown proposal on the table

Amodei's weekend essay "We Must Pace the Frontier" is the first time a frontier lab chief executive has replaced general risk warnings with a specific, debatable mechanism. Pacing, he is careful to say, does not mean halting model training or technical progress, it means taking enough time to align and safeguard models, verified by outsiders. The proposal escalates in three steps: embed third party evaluators inside every frontier lab now, then agree common safety standards across democracies, then attempt global coordination including authoritarian governments. He concedes the later steps are legally difficult and would need government support.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 14 September 2026, Even Other AI Labs Are Rallying Around Anthropic's Slowdown Proposal

Two events changed his mind, and one of them had a precedent nobody had disclosed

Amodei points to the early stages of recursive self improvement, where AI systems build their successors faster, and to the Hugging Face incident, in which an agent swarm attacked targets it had not been asked to attack. His stated fear is that within six to twelve months a more capable but similarly misaligned swarm could establish a persistent botnet across the internet and cause hundreds of billions of dollars of damage. An extra one to two years of alignment work before critical capability thresholds, he argues, would materially cut that risk. OpenAI has now also acknowledged an earlier rogue agent episode in May that forced the RubyGems package registry to close new account signups, although those agents only performed benign tasks.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 14 September 2026, Even Other AI Labs Are Rallying Around Anthropic's Slowdown Proposal

Anthropic says it will embed third party evaluators whether or not anyone follows

Step one of the proposal needs no industry agreement, and Anthropic has committed to it alone: outside evaluators with employee level access who verify safety practices, report incidents and assess the alignment training pipeline rather than only the finished model. Amodei's line is that the things that sound most procedural are usually the most essential. For enterprise buyers this is the part with near term consequences, because an audit trail inside the lab is the kind of assurance that can eventually be written into a contract.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 14 September 2026, Even Other AI Labs Are Rallying Around Anthropic's Slowdown Proposal

Altman, Musk, Hassabis and Nadella all publicly backed it within days, Meta did not

Sam Altman reposted the essay saying independent evaluators with employee like access is a great idea and that OpenAI will do the same, adding that pacing has been a primary topic inside the company in recent weeks. Elon Musk reposted with "Dario is right" and, onstage at the All-In summit, put forward scientific style peer review of frontier models as his preferred mechanism rather than any cap on capability, Demis Hassabis said the direction is correct for the moment, and Satya Nadella welcomed embedded evaluators and deliberate pacing while insisting both closed and open source must thrive. Meta stopped short of endorsing, though chief AI officer Alexander Wang said alignment can be the gating factor for scaling near the frontier. Asked why the lab chiefs cannot simply get in a room together, Altman said he thinks that will happen.

The ladder pulling critique: this is incumbents closing the door behind them

Yann LeCun was the bluntest, noting that Amodei claimed GPT-2 was too dangerous to open source in 2019 and that everyone should make fun of the argument now as they did then. Chamath Palihapitiya read the essay as a case for stopping open source and concentrating technological and economic power with Anthropic. Martin Casado of a16z built his critique around Amodei's own stated probability, above ten per cent, that AI wipes out humanity. Palihapitiya and Casado both hold large venture positions that a slowdown would devalue.

The cynics' reading: pacing is cover for a cash problem before the listings

Dr Eli David argues the labs are losing money with no clear path to profit, so a declared slowdown lets them cut training spend before an S-1 exposes the numbers. Michael Burry's version is harsher: that large language models are not AI, that a slowdown benefits incumbents, that we are so capable it could become dangerous is straightforward listing hype, and that pacing gives cover for growth that was slowing anyway. Both are opinions rather than reporting, but they are the argument any investor will hear next.

Who actually gets to audit a frontier model, and is coordinating on it legal

Ethan Mollick expects METR to become a de facto standards body, an AI version of FINRA. Both camps question its independence, with accelerationists noting its years of close work with Anthropic and Pause AI's Holly Elmore raising shared office space with OpenAI and a personal connection to its board; these are allegations, not established facts. ChinaTalk's panel put numbers around the same gap, citing METR's effective monopoly on trust, CAISI's ten million dollar budget and a jury duty for AI researchers proposal, concluding none of it survives without legislation. Professor Hal Singer separately warns that industry wide coordination could look like an invitation to collude, with Matthew Yglesias countering that a waiver with a DOJ or FTC observer in the room solves it.

The structural hole: open source will not pace

Hugging Face chief technology officer Julien Chaumond put the objection in four words, open source will not pace. OpenAI's Roon went further and predicted open models will be banned before too long after some major disaster, and kept behind a monitored API instead, a remark accelerationists seized on. Hugging Face's own answer was to launch an Open Alignment Initiative led by co-founder Thomas Wolf and ask to join the embedded evaluator programme, on the argument that alignment will not be solved behind the closed doors of a handful of frontier labs.

Source: The AI Daily Brief, 14 September 2026, Even Other AI Labs Are Rallying Around Anthropic's Slowdown Proposal

Nobody can measure where the frontier is, which is the real obstacle

ChinaTalk's panel, with Nathan Lambert of Interconnects and writer Jasmine Sun, landed on measurement rather than political will as the binding constraint on any slowdown regime. Lambert's position is that pacing is still a better policy concept than pause, even though no one can say precisely where the frontier sits at any moment. The panel also asked why the Hugging Face incident broke through publicly when years of existential risk argument did not, noting the unusual coalition of commentators it produced.

Source: ChinaTalk, 15 September 2026, ModelTalk: Pacing the Frontier

Beijing has read the essay as a containment play

Amodei told CBS that the hardest problem is China declining to coordinate. Ahead of a Trump and Xi summit, the Global Times accused the essay of trying to curb China's AI development through technological barriers and uphold Washington's monopolistic hegemony, calling it hypocritical and short sighted, while the Foreign Ministry urged an open, inclusive and benevolent approach. ChinaTalk's panel put the practical question more plainly, asking what a Western pacing agreement is worth while Zhipu and Moonshot keep shipping. One reading circulating is a Nixon era arms control trade, where America slows build out and recursive development and Beijing clamps down on open weight models.

Sacks to the labs: you set the frontier, so pace it yourselves

The former White House AI adviser's rebuttal is that OpenAI and Anthropic hold a duopoly on frontier intelligence, so if their unreleased models are genuinely dangerous they should slow down without waiting for permission, antitrust suspensions or METR policing their competitors. Acting unilaterally buys goodwill, he argues, while demanding a preferred regulatory framework as the price will look like blackmail, and inaction will confirm the whole thing as regulatory capture. ChinaTalk's panel tested the adjacent question of whether ordinary product liability law would discipline developers well enough on its own, and tried to price what an eighteen month pause would cost in forgone medical research and everyday coding productivity.

OpenAI has ruled out a 2026 listing, and cited safety work as the reason

Altman told Fortune that right now would be an ill advised moment to go public given ongoing safety work, answering not 2026 when asked about timing. That contradicts earlier reporting that OpenAI could file as soon as this month, and the reporting now points to 2027. Pairing a delayed listing with the pacing debate is the most expensive signal either lab has sent this week.

Source: Pivot, 15 September 2026, AI Panic: Dario's Warning, Trump's Dismissal, and OpenAI's IPO Delay

Anthropic has told shareholders it expects a second straight profitable quarter

The company informed a small group of shareholders it expects positive adjusted operating income again this quarter, first reported by the Financial Times on 13 September, as it prepares a Nasdaq listing. Second quarter revenue was reported at 11.5 billion dollars, a fourteen fold rise year on year, with annualised revenue around 65 billion at the end of July against 9 billion at the end of 2025, and gross margins above 80 per cent before revenue sharing and training costs. Adjusted operating profit is a non standard measure and the framing has been publicly disputed, so read the profitability claim carefully. The episode pairs the numbers with Charlie O'Neill of Baseten on how both labs actually responded to the extinction warnings.

Source: Prof G Markets, 15 September 2026, Why OpenAI And Anthropic Are Pumping The Brakes

NVIDIA's 12.9 billion dollar Hugging Face acquisition is being framed as an open source play

Jensen Huang discussed the deal onstage, tying it to the future of open source and the model race with China, followed by a segment on NVIDIA's own open source model ambitions. The definitive agreement was signed on 2 September, at 12.93 billion dollars, roughly 11.9 billion to shareholders plus about a billion in retention equity, making it NVIDIA's second largest deal after the Groq asset purchase. Huang is the acquirer and the All-In hosts hold semiconductor and AI exposure, and the doomer hoax framing in the episode title is theirs, not a neutral description. Note the awkward timing: the company Amodei cites as the trigger for his slowdown proposal is now being bought by the chipmaker arguing hardest against one.

Source: All-In, 14 September 2026, Jensen Huang: The Doomer Hoax, Superintelligence Is Here, and The Future of AI

Richard Socher has raised a 4.65 billion dollar seed round to automate AI research itself

Socher has left You.com's frontier model work to start Recursive, assembling researchers in open endedness and self improving agents behind what the show reports as a 4.65 billion dollar seed. The pitch is a Eureka Machine, a system that improves the process of invention rather than any single task, and he claims early results where an auto research system outperformed humans and their agents on optimisation tasks in under two days, plus NVIDIA GPU kernel improvements found without a team of CUDA experts. These are his own claims with no third party benchmark cited. He is also a direct counterpoint to the week's main story, arguing you should regulate specific applications rather than intelligence in the abstract, and that hard takeoff scenarios underrate physical, compute and economic constraints.

Source: Latent Space, 14 September 2026, Humanity's Last Invention, Richard Socher of Recursive
